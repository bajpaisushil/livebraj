# vamsi-vat

*Researched and independently verified 2026-09-27. Confidence: partial.*

## oneLine

Vamsi Vat (Banshivat Maharaas Sthali) is a small walled courtyard temple built round an old banyan in the Bansi-bat mohalla of Vrindavan — the spot where Krishna is said to have played the flute to call the gopis and where the Maharas took place — and although every text from the Bhakti-ratnakara onward calls it "on the bank of the Yamuna", it stands today about 430 m inland from the river's mapped channel, behind houses, entered from a narrow lane.

## scale

PRECINCT: "an area of approx. 30,000 sq feet" = ~2,790 m², from the mahants via banshivat.org.in. Consistent with a plot of roughly 70 × 40 m. That is the one hard figure.

Everything below is photo-derived. I re-scaled independently against standing and seated adults at ~1.65-1.70 m, door heights at ~2.0-2.2 m, and the chequerboard tiles reading ~0.35-0.40 m square. Treat as ±20%. Where I differ from the account I was checking, the difference is marked.

- Outer gateway: ~6.5 m wide overall; clear arch opening ~2.8-3.0 m; arch crown ~4.0 m; cornice slab ~4.5-5.0 m; top of the central stucco shikhara finial ~7.5-8.0 m. Tallest point on the site. Engaged columns ~0.8-1.0 m diameter. (Unchanged — confirmed against a man walking through the arch.)
- Forecourt: CHANGED. Not a 4 m walk. A broad irregular yard roughly 20-30 m across between the gate and the inner doorway, part flagged in pale stone, part earth. Surrounding buildings one to two storeys, ~4-8 m.
- Inner paved courtyard (parisar): ~18-22 m × 14-18 m. The "L-shaped" plan in the original account is not demonstrable from any photograph — treat the outline as unknown and keep it roughly rectangular-irregular.
- Main tree plinth (rectangular): ~5-6 m × ~4 m, 0.8-0.9 m high.
- Second tree platform (polygonal/near-circular, ashta-sakhi panels): ~5-6 m across, ~1.0-1.2 m high, ten to twelve facets.
- The banyan: trunk mass ~2.5-3.0 m across at the plinth; height ~12-15 m; canopy spread ~15-18 m, filling the court.
- Red sandstone shrine on the plinth: ~0.9-1.0 m square × ~1.6-1.8 m tall to the tip of the bud finial.
- Charan Mandir: canopy ~3.0-3.5 m square on marble piers, ~3.5-4.0 m to the top of the slab; sinhasan within ~1.6 m wide × ~2.4 m high; marble platform ~3.0-3.5 m × ~2.5 m, two steps. Charan slab ~0.9 × 0.6 m under glass.
- Tiered stone lamp-tower: ~0.8 m diameter × ~1.2-1.4 m tall including its square base.
- Lantern kiosk on a pillar: kiosk ~0.8 m across × ~0.9 m tall, pillar ~1.3 m; total ~2.2-2.3 m.
- Large (Oel) mandir: ~11-13 m frontage; depth UNKNOWN (no photograph shows the flank or rear — the original account's 8-10 m is a guess and should be labelled as one); plinth 0.5-0.6 m (4-5 steps); cornice ~4.0-4.2 m; parapet top ~5.0-5.5 m; flat accessible roof.
- Courtyard boundary walls: CHANGED. ~2.6-3.2 m, not 3.5-4.5 m. Measured against a standing man and a seated man in two panoramas, the couplet panels top out near 2.8-3.0 m and the surrounding single-storey eaves sit just above that.
- Painted gopi frieze band: CHANGED. It sits ON the floor — roughly 0.05 m to 0.95-1.00 m above the paving, a skirting frieze. Not 0.6-1.4 m.
- Lean-to canopy over part of the court: steel posts, underside of the sheet at ~3.0-3.2 m.
- Approach lane outside the gate: ~3-5 m wide, buildings 1-2 storeys, ~4-8 m.
- Forecourt inscription stele: slab ~1.6 m wide × ~1.6 m high, flanking columns to ~2.4 m, on a plinth ~0.9 m; overall ~2.6 m × ~2.6 m.

So: a 1:1 model still needs nothing taller than ~8 m, and almost everything is between 3 and 5.5 m — lower than the previous account had it, because the courtyard walls come down by about a metre.

## architecture

Single-storey walled precinct, whitewashed and cream lime-plastered thin brick, every roof flat with a parapet. Not a tower temple. The original researcher's account is broadly sound and I confirmed most of it from photographs; the corrections below are the parts that were wrong or conflated.

SEQUENCE FROM THE STREET

1. APPROACH LANE, ~3-5 m wide, part brick-paved part earth, one- and two-storey plastered brick houses in poor repair, external stairs to flat roofs, cows. A Pushtimarg Baithak of Vallabhacharya is at Banshi Vat (it is one of the 84 Baithaks; sources disagree whether it is the 4th or the 32nd). CORRECTION: in Pushtimarg a baithak enshrines a gaddi (cushioned seat), not an image. The "small seated stone acharya figure at its street corner" is unverified and appears to be a conflation with the Vyasa/Shukadeva niche inside the court. Do not model a seated acharya on the lane unless you have a photograph of it.

2. OUTER GATEWAY — confirmed exactly, in photographs from 2009, 2012 and 2020. Cream/whitewashed stucco. Two heavy engaged round columns with fluted/reeded shafts on square pedestals, square abaci. One large cusped (multifoil) arch. A thick flat cornice slab runs right across above it. On the slab sit three stucco finials: two large ribbed, gadrooned dome-like mounds flanking a taller central stepped miniature nagara shikhara with amalaka and kalash. These three are the only vertical or curved forms in the whole complex. Overhead electricity wires cross in front of it.
   THE SIGN HAS CHANGED. Older (2009-2012): a white board, red lettering, Devanagari "मन्दिर ठा. श्री बंशीवट बिहारी जी महाराज / बंशीवट महारास स्थली" with Roman "MANDIR TH. SHREE BANSHIBAT BIHARI JI MAHARAJ / BANSHIBAT MAHARAS STHALEE". Current (photographed 2020): a GREEN board reading "बंशीवट मन्दिर (महारास स्थली) / BANSHIVAT TAMPLE (MAHARAS STHALI)". The misspelling "TAMPLE" is real and is on the green board. Both spellings are wrong in different ways; pick the era you are modelling.

3. FORECOURT — CORRECTED. Not "a straight paved walk ~4 m wide with grass verges". It is a broad, irregular open yard roughly 20-30 m across, part laid in large pale stone flags (recent), part bare earth. Round it: a decayed two-storey building with an external staircase and a blue door; a raised masonry platform with steps where people sit; single-storey ochre ranges with segmental-arched openings and metal shutters; cylindrical woven-reed tree guards; cows. A maroon metal double gate in one side wall opens to another lane.
   In this yard stands a large inscription monument — CORRECTED: pale buff/pink SANDSTONE, a tall inscribed slab flanked by two engaged round columns with ball finials, flat-topped, on a heavy moulded plinth with carved rosette and bead panels. It is NOT capped by a pyramidal pavilion roof. The pyramidal roof the original account attached to it belongs to a DIFFERENT, separate object: a small square kiosk with a red-painted pyramidal roof standing immediately beside the inner doorway, carrying its own inscription slab. Two things, not one.

4. INNER DOORWAY into the courtyard — confirmed. A cusped ogee arch in a cream wall banded with green, blue and ochre, under a flat concrete chhajja, the arch head outlined in green. Above it a yellow/ochre board with red Devanagari: "ठा. श्री बंशीवट बिहारी जी महाराज / अखिल कोटि ब्रह्माण्ड नायक भगवान श्रीकृष्ण की महारास स्थली (महारास मंडल)". The leaves are tall studded timber double doors, the inner faces painted with panels reading "राधा राधा राधा". A 2006 photograph shows the same opening as a plain round-headed arch with peeling red bands — it was remodelled into the cusped form between 2006 and 2012.

5. THE COURTYARD (parisar). Floor entirely black-and-white chequerboard marble laid DIAGONALLY with a border — confirmed in every photograph from 2006 to 2020. Hemmed in on all sides by single-storey ranges; the enclosing walls and parapets are LOWER than the original account says (see scale).
   THE MAIN TREE PLINTH: rectangular, plastered masonry. Faced in dark maroon/plum marble in 2006-2012; by 2020 re-faced in pink/terracotta render with a white coping and painted with peacocks, a large pink lotus, a yellow crescent moon with a face, and standing gopis. The original account is right that it has been re-faced at least once.
   A SECOND TREE PLATFORM exists that the original account missed: a POLYGONAL (roughly ten- to twelve-sided, reading as circular) drum, cream and pink, with a moulded scalloped base and coping, each facet carrying a painted standing sakhi in an oval frame with her name above in Devanagari — the ashta-sakhi. Photographed 2019 on the same chequerboard floor. Do not merge it with the rectangular plinth.
   ONE OR TWO CARVED RED SANDSTONE MINIATURE SHRINES stand on the corner of the main plinth (some years show a pair side by side). Each: pierced jali panels, a cusped-arch niche flanked by engaged colonnettes with carved foliate spandrels, a flat projecting cornice slab, and above it a short stepped drum carrying a fluted, gadrooned amalaka-like bud finial. CORRECTION: it is a cornice-and-finial top, not a stepped pyramidal roof. Aerial roots of the banyan wrap round it.
   A RUINED FRESCO STRUCTURE STANDS IN THE COURT, not in the back garden as the original account placed it: a decayed brick core with a moulded scalloped (lotus-petal) cornice band, against which lean salvaged plaster panels bearing life-size gopi figures in an older, finer style; two round stone drums (old column bases or grinding stones) lie in front. It is immediately beside the tree and the Charan Mandir.
   A TIERED STONE LAMP-TOWER (deepmala): a circular drum with four or five tiers of small rectangular lamp niches on a square moulded base, free-standing on the chequerboard floor. Missed by the original account.
   A SMALL STONE LANTERN KIOSK ON A PILLAR: octagonal, pierced cusped-arch openings on each face, a conical/pyramidal cap with a knob finial, raised on a plain pillar. Also missed.
   AN OLDER ENCLOSED WHITE MARBLE KIOSK, jali-sided with a cusped-arch front on a stepped black-and-white banded base, appears in 2006-2012 photographs standing near the tree. It is a separate structure from the Charan Mandir canopy.

6. THE CHARAN MANDIR — the original account is CORRECT and I confirmed it. An open white marble canopy on carved marble piers, with a cusped marble arch and a carved cornice/valance across the front, standing on a two-step white marble platform with a black-and-white chequered apron. Inside it a dark timber-and-gilt sinhasan with a gilded cusped valance holds the Maharas pichwai. The marble Charan slab is set in the floor in front, under glass. It stands immediately beside the tree plinth under the sheet canopy — not free in the centre of the court. The present arrangement was installed in August 2006 (the temple's own gallery documents the sthapan).

7. THE LARGE MANDIR (the "King Oyal/Oel" building) — confirmed in detail. Single-storey, cream plaster. Three round-headed arched openings in the centre with maroon/wine timber doors and vertical-bar grille panels, each flanked outward by a recessed panel holding a maroon double door under a blind arch. A painted band of green/teal rosettes and red-ochre scrolls runs over the arches. A deep projecting cornice on close-set small brackets, a low parapet, a flat accessible roof. Raised on a plinth of about half a metre reached by two short flights of four or five steps with curved plastered cheek-walls painted with a blue stripe. The plinth face carries the painted gopi frieze. Inside: bright Braj-folk murals (Radha and Krishna under a parasol with gopis, Krishna with cowherds) on a blue ground, and a sanctum door frame painted green with red and white bands, foliage scroll, rosettes and small dwarapala figure panels at the base. A wall clock hangs above the sanctum door. The temple's own site dates this building: "The other large mandir was built by King Oyal (from Lucknow) in the 1900's."

8. A LEAN-TO CANOPY of corrugated/metal sheet on steel posts covers part of the court. The posts are painted dark red in most years, pale in others.

9. COURTYARD WALLS. Whitewashed, marble-clad in places. At the base, sitting ON the floor, a continuous painted frieze on a blue ground: dancing gopis alternating with blue Krishnas holding hands, over the blue Yamuna with floating lotus petals, on a dark green ground line. Above the frieze, framed panels of Sanskrit and Hindi couplets in red and blue lettering (one quotes the Gita Govinda "dhira-samira yamuna-tire"), with inset white marble inscription tablets. In places the painting extends much higher into a full landscape: a forest of flowering trees with TWO PEACOCKS above the blue river band, and large figural panels of Krishna with the flute and Radha enthroned. Wall niches closed with maroon timber-and-glass doors hold small marble figures.

10. A FURTHER ROOFED COURT appears in the temple's own gallery: a cream range with about seven cusped-arched openings with orange/red frames and yellow cloth valances, under a translucent/corrugated roof spanning the whole court, chequerboard floor. Treat as part of the complex; its position relative to the main court is not established.

11. BEHIND/NORTH: a large walled plot of old thin-brick (lakhauri) walls, corrugated sheds, scattered ruins and stacked salvaged brick and timber, neem and babul trees, bare earth. A boundary wall with MERLON/battlement coping and a small corner kiosk with a pyramidal roof — CORRECTION: these are plastered masonry, maroon-painted dado with whitewash above and a red-painted roof, NOT red sandstone. Carved buff sandstone remains lie about, including a large deeply fluted column drum on a moulded base. Old wall paintings survive on the decayed structures here — Kaliya-damana (Krishna dancing on the serpent), Krishna and Radha on a takht, Krishna and gopis — in an older, finer Jaipur/Braj style than the modern frieze. Beyond this ground the land falls away toward the Yamuna floodplain. The temple's own directions say the Parikrama Marg reaches "the back side" of the place.

THE TREE: one heavy banyan, trunk a fused mass of aerial roots, red and orange cloth tied round it, canopy filling the court and pressing against the surrounding roofs. Seen from a neighbouring roof it reads as a single spreading crown about as wide as the court. In tradition the present tree is a replant: Vajranabha planted the original, the Yamuna in flood carried it away, and about 550 years ago Madhu Pandita (a disciple of Gadadhara Pandita) took a branch and planted it here — which is itself the tradition's own explanation of why the site is now inland.

STILL THIN: no measured plan, no elevation, no survey. Interior depths of the large mandir are unknown — no photograph shows the building from the side or rear. The overall outline of the court, the relation of the second (polygonal) tree platform to the main one, and the position of the seven-arched roofed court are not established. The founding date is nowhere recorded.

## distinguishing

First, and confirmed in photographs from 2006 to 2020: a heavy fused-root banyan on a low painted masonry plinth in the middle of a small enclosed court whose floor is entirely BLACK-AND-WHITE CHEQUERBOARD MARBLE LAID DIAGONALLY, and round the walls, sitting directly on that floor, a continuous painted frieze of dancing gopis alternating with blue Krishnas holding hands above the blue Yamuna with floating lotus petals — a rasa-mandala painted as a skirting band right round the court and along the plinth face of the main temple. Above it, framed Hindi and Sanskrit couplets in red and blue, and in places the painting carries on upward into a forest landscape with two peacocks. (I have softened the original account's claim that nowhere else in Vrindavan does this occur — it may well be unique but that is not something the sources establish.)

Second, and the surest single identifier: the outer gate. A plain whitewashed stucco arch — one large cusped multifoil opening between two fat fluted engaged round columns — carrying a thick flat cornice slab, and standing on that slab three stucco finials: two ribbed, gadrooned dome-like mounds flanking a taller stepped miniature shikhara with amalaka and kalash. It is about 7.5-8 m tall and is the tallest thing on the site. The current green board on it reads "BANSHIVAT TAMPLE (MAHARAS STHALI)" — the misspelling is on the real sign, verified in a 2020 photograph. An earlier white board read "BANSHIBAT ... STHALEE".

Third: the carved red sandstone miniature shrine on the corner of the tree plinth, roughly knee-to-chest height, with pierced jali, a cusped-arch niche between engaged colonnettes, a flat cornice slab and a fluted bud finial, with the banyan's aerial roots growing round it — beside the small open white marble canopy on marble piers holding a dark-and-gilt sinhasan with the Maharas pichwai, the Charan slab under glass in the floor in front.

Fourth, easy to overlook and worth building because nothing else looks like it: the polygonal, near-circular plinth painted with the named ashta-sakhi in panels, and the tiered circular stone lamp-tower with four or five rows of little lamp niches standing free on the chequerboard.

## doNotBuild

1. NOT A GHAT. The literature is unanimous that it was on the bank — Bhakti-ratnakara 5/2379-81 ("wonderfully beautiful Vamsivat on the bank of the Yamuna"), Chaitanya-charitamrita Adi 1.17 ("vamsivata-tata-sthitah"), and the Chaurasi Pada verse Growse translates ("Near the Bansi-bat, a sweetly pretty spot... on the bank of the Jamuna's stream"). It is not on the bank now. The temple's own site: "as time passed land between the two places became divided, because the sacred Yamuna River has been receding very fast." Build no steps into water, no river frontage, no bathing ghat. The mapped Yamuna centreline is 428 m NNW.
   BUT DO NOT BUILD THE RIVER VIEW EITHER. The original account said the Yamuna "is visible from the temple's roof terrace as a pale band beyond trees". I could not verify that. The two rooftop photographs in the temple's own gallery show flat roofs, corrugated sheds, trees and distant temple spires — no river. Leave it out.

2. NO SHIKHARA over the sanctum, no dome, no gopuram, no curved Bengali roof. Every roof on site is flat with a parapet. The only tower-like forms are the three small stucco finials, and they are on the GATE.

3. NOT RED SANDSTONE MONUMENTAL ARCHITECTURE. Do not model it like Govind Dev, Madan Mohan or Jugal Kishore. Whitewashed and cream lime-plastered thin brick, maroon/wine-painted timber joinery, blue and ochre lines. Stone on site is limited to: the red sandstone miniature shrine(s) on the tree plinth; a buff sandstone inscription stele in the forecourt; loose carved sandstone remains and a fluted column drum in the back plot; the white marble of the Charan canopy, the niche linings and the floor.
   CORRECTION to the original account: the back-garden enclosure wall with merlon coping and its corner kiosk are NOT red sandstone. They are plastered masonry, maroon dado and whitewash, with a red-painted pyramidal roof on the kiosk.

4. DO NOT PUT IT ON THE PARIKRAMA MARG FRONTAGE. Its gate opens on a narrow inner lane; the nearest point of Parikram Marg (OSM way 16707911) is 79 m NNW, reached by a back lane.

5. DO NOT BUILD A FREE-STANDING BANYAN IN A RAILED PARK ON GRASS, and do not merge the two Vanshivats. Bhandirvan's Vanshivat is a different site about 10 km away in Mant tehsil, where the Radha-Krishna marriage by Brahma is placed. This confusion is live on Wikimedia Commons itself: the files "Vanshivat tree.jpg" and "Radha Krishna in Vanshivat.jpg" (the latter described "Image clicked at Vanshivat temple, Vrindavan") are BOTH filed under Category:Radha Krishna Vivah Sthali, Bhandirvan. Trust neither the filename nor the description on such images.

6. NOT A SPRAWLING MULTI-TRUNK BANYAN GROVE. One heavy fused-root trunk on a plinth, pressed against the surrounding buildings.

7. DO NOT BUILD FOUR MATCHING CORNER TEMPLES. The source is located and quoted: vrindavanforest.com, reproducing Sri Vraja-mandala Parikrama — "There are four very small temples in each of the four corners of the quadrangular wall of Vamsivata. The deities of Sri Ramanujacarya, Sri Madhvacarya, Sri Visnusvami and Sri Nimbarkacarya were established within them. Nowadays, some other deities have replaced them." No photograph shows a matched set of four at the corners. What the photographs do show is a scatter of small shrines, kiosks and wall niches with maroon timber-and-glass doors. Build those; treat the four corner temples as unverified.

8. NOT SAFFRON/ORANGE PAINTED BUILDINGS. Whitewash and cream, maroon-red doors and frames, thin blue and ochre outlining, bright polychrome folk murals low down, black-and-white marble floor. (Individual stone images — the Hanuman relief — are painted orange; the architecture is not.)

9. NO LARGE ASSEMBLY HALL OR OPEN RASA PLAZA. ~30,000 sq ft in total, but the paved court with the tree is small and hemmed in, and the large mandir is one single-storey range of 11-13 m frontage.

10. DO NOT GIVE THE FOUNDING A DATE — but do not black out the two dates that ARE recorded. No source states when Shri Nivasacharya founded it. The temple's own site does state that Girdhari Sharn Dev acharya took over the worship "in 1820", and that the large mandir "was built by King Oyal (from Lucknow) in the 1900's". Use those two; invent nothing else.

11. DO NOT PRESENT THE SECTARIAN HISTORY AS SETTLED. The temple's own site says it "was founded by the Acharya of Nimbark sampradaya - Shri Nivasachary". Sri Vraja-mandala Parikrama says instead that "Gaudiya Vaisnavas used to serve here, but then Brahmacariji, the spiritual guide of a king of Gvaliyor, bought this place. Since then, it has been in the care of the Nimbarka sampradaya." Those are different stories. Also note the name trap: Srinivasacharya of the Nimbarka sampradaya (Nimbarka's own disciple, its second acharya) is NOT the Gaudiya Srinivasa Acharya (disciple of Gopala Bhatta). The temple means the Nimbarki one.

12. DO NOT ATTRIBUTE THE VERSE TO SUR DAS. This was an error in the account I was checking. The pada Growse translates ("Near the Bansi-bat, a sweetly pretty spot...") is from the CHAURASI PADA of HIT HARIVANSH, founder of the Radhavallabha sampradaya — Growse prints it under that heading, with the poet's signature line "Rejoice, Sri Hit Hari Vans!" in every verse, and goes on to explain that "Hari Vans assumed the title of Hit Ji". Sur Das is not the author and has nothing to do with it.

13. DO NOT BUILD A FORMAL ENTRANCE AVENUE. No 4 m walk with grass verges. A broad irregular yard, part flagged, part earth, with decayed buildings, a raised platform, tree guards and cows.

14. DO NOT PUT THE GOPI FRIEZE AT DADO HEIGHT. It is a skirting frieze standing on the floor.

## deities

Thakur Shri Banshivat Bihari Ji Maharaj — the name on both signboards. In the large (Oel) mandir: Radha and Krishna, small dressed metal murtis on a cloth-draped marble altar between marble pillars, with a smaller Krishna in front; the Krishna murti is brass/gilt with silver ornament, crowned, flute in hand. In the Charan Mandir: no murti but a white marble slab set flush in a black marble floor under glass, incised with two pairs of feet labelled in Hindi श्री कृष्ण चरण (left) and श्री राधिका चरण (right), with the Maharas pichwai above it in a gilt timber sinhasan. A second, separate charan exists elsewhere in the precinct: a dark stone slab bearing about fourteen pairs of footprints set in a pink sandstone recess — do not merge the two. Courtyard wall niches, closed with maroon timber-and-glass doors, hold small white marble figures; one is labelled in red Hindi on the wall above "वेद व्यास जी (बैठे हुये) / शुक देव जी दण्डवत करते हुये" — Vedavyasa seated with Shukadeva prostrate on the step below. A Hanuman relief painted orange and stuck with silver foil stands in another niche. The Gopinatha deity that Madhu Pandita is said to have found under this tree is not here: He went to the Radha Gopinath temple 385 m west and thence to Jaipur.

- NOTE ON METHOD: the architectural description in the account under review was almost entirely right, and I have said so where it is. What I changed, I changed because I looked at the photographs myself rather than at descriptions of them. The six substantive corrections are: the Sur Das misattribution (it is Hit Harivansh), the frieze height (it sits on the floor), the courtyard wall height (~2.6-3.2 m, not 3.5-4.5 m), the forecourt (a broad yard, not an avenue with verges), the forecourt inscription monument (two separate objects conflated into one), and the back-garden wall (plastered masonry, not red sandstone). Two features were missed entirely and are worth building: the polygonal ashta-sakhi plinth and the tiered stone lamp-tower. One claim could not be verified and should be dropped: the Yamuna visible from the roof.
