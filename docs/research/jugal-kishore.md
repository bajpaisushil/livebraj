# jugal-kishore — exterior survey

*Researched 2026-09-28 by one agent and independently checked by a second whose brief was to refute it. Labels are the researcher's own: MEASURED / SOURCED / DERIVED / ESTIMATED / UNKNOWN.*

*Survey and checker are paired by the workflow journal's launch key, not by completion order — see BACKLOG 2026-09-30 for why that matters.*

## What it is

Shri Jugal Kishor (ASI N-UP-A196, 1627 CE), Kesi Ghat, Vrindavan, is a two-cell red-sandstone ruin — a flat-roofed, almost windowless cubical ardha-mandap in front and, behind it, an octagonal shrine whose angles are cut away until it reads circular, carrying a tall, steep, fluted near-conical sikhara crowned by a ribbed amalaka; the nave that once stood in front of it is completely gone, the building is empty and padlocked, and its tower is the one landmark of the Keshi Ghat skyline.

## The silhouette

WHAT SURVIVES (confirmed 2015 photograph and an August 2026 Hindi news report): two masses only, on an east–west axis.

(a) EAST — the ardha-mandap (Growse calls it the "choir"). A cube of ashlar with a bold projecting cornice, a pendant/leaf frieze and a small figural arcade band above it, a low parapet, and behind the parapet a LOW SLAB ROOF, slightly ridged, rising a little towards the shrine. It carries NO tower, NO dome, NO chhatri, NO finial. Flat-topped. [SOURCED: Burgess 1910 p.158 "Above the level of the mandap roof the sikhara tapers upwards"; MEASURED from Growse's autotype plate and from the 2015 river photograph.]

(b) WEST — the shrine (cella) and its sikhara. Burgess: the cella is "about 16 ft. square inside… outside it is octagonal in plan with the angles broken up so as to make it almost circular." From the 2015 photograph this is exactly right: eight broad flat faces, and at each of the eight corners a cluster of about three narrow half-round roll mouldings, so that the silhouette is effectively CIRCULAR. Model it with ≥24 radial segments, not 8.

The sikhara profile, MEASURED off the 2015 photograph and scaled on the plan-derived 9.9 m diameter at the mandap-roof ring (h = 0 at that ring, D = outside diameter):
  h = −4.6 m  D ≈ 9.3 m   (lower drum, below a bold ring)
  h =  0.0 m  D ≈ 9.9 m   (projecting ring at mandap-roof / cornice level — widest point)
  h = +4.0 m  D ≈ 9.0 m   (1st string course)
  h = +7.2 m  D ≈ 7.1 m   (2nd string course)
  h = +9.9 m  D ≈ 5.0 m   (3rd string course)
  h = +11.8 m D ≈ 2.4 m   (neck / griva, after a fast inward shoulder)
  h = +12.3 m D ≈ 3.65 m  (amalaka, maximum)
  h = +14.2 m             (top)
Exactly THREE string courses above the mandap roof — which is precisely what Burgess states — plus one ring at roof level and one lower on the drum. The profile is a steep near-cone with only slight convexity in the lowest stage; it is NOT a latina Nagara S-curve and NOT bulbous.

WHAT CROWNS IT, stated plainly: an AMALAKA. A flattened, strongly ribbed stone disc roughly 3.6 m across and ~1.9 m tall, overhanging a short fluted neck that sits on a cluster of 3–4 horizontal roll mouldings. Roughly 9–10 lobes are countable across the front half, so about 18–20 ribs in the round. On top of the amalaka is a LOW, SMOOTH, SLIGHTLY DOMED CAP. Nothing else. No kalasha, no pot, no gold, no trident, no flagstaff, no pennant, in the 1880s autotype, in the 1910 plate, or in the 2015 photograph. [Burgess 1910 p.158: "crowned by an amalasila."]

WHAT IS LOST: the nave / outer porch, entirely. Growse (p. 254): "The nave has been completely destroyed." Burgess (p. 158): "the outer porch has entirely disappeared, and what is left is only the ardha-mandap and shrine." The 1910 ASI plan shows only a low platform-and-steps stub, ~2.6 m deep, east of the mandap's east wall. Also lost: large areas of the mandap's ashlar facing, exposing the rubble/brick core — a big ragged patch immediately around and above the great east arch in both 19th-century photographs. Whether ASI has since refaced it is UNKNOWN; no modern photograph of the east front was found.

Small square holes (putlog or vent) are scattered over the sikhara faces in all three photographs — keep them, they read at distance.

## The elevation

THE GOVERNING FACT: this building is about 95–97% SOLID. Almost every "opening" you can see is a blind niche or a carved stone panel. Do not open it up.

EAST FRONT of the ardha-mandap (one very tall storey; this is now the main face, because the arch that reads as the facade was originally the internal choir arch onto the vanished nave):

1. Plinth. Stepped mouldings, four to six courses, with a flight of steps set on the FLANK (visible bottom-left in the Growse autotype) rather than on axis. In the 1880s the plinth emerges from a bare earth mound with the ground falling away on the flank.

2. The wall, ~8–10 m of unbroken ashlar to the cornice. Openings on this face: ONE doorway, ~1.3–1.6 m wide by ~2.8–3.3 m high (ESTIMATED from the photographs) — roughly 4–5 m² of void in a face of roughly 79 m², i.e. an opening:solid ratio of about 1:15 (≈6% open). Flanking it at low level, two BLIND niches each side with cusped/trefoil heads, deeply framed, each ~0.4 × 0.9 m — these are recesses, not holes.

3. The doorway surround. A heavy rectangular architrave studded with rows of disc rosettes down both jambs and across the lintel — the rosettes are the single most repeated motif on the building. Directly above the lintel, a band of about nine small cusped arched niches, a miniature blind arcade.

4. THE GREAT ARCH. A single broad arch, nearly semicircular with a faint point at the crown, springing at roughly half wall height, spanning most of the bay (≈4.5–5.5 m, ESTIMATED), formed of large voussoir-like blocks. Its head is not an opening: it is filled with a carved stone screen. Growse p. 254: "The choir arch is an interesting composition with a fan-light, so to speak, of pierced tracery in the head of the arch, and a group above representing Krishna supporting the Gobardhan hill." In the photograph you can read, from the bottom up: a semicircular fan of radiating pierced ribs sitting on the lintel band; above it a central figural/foliate group with scrolled makara-like volutes; four large disc rosettes at the corners of the tympanum; and a small stupa-like finial at the apex of the inner frame.

5. Cornice zone, three bands: a boldly projecting moulded cornice with a bead-and-dentil undercourse; above it a frieze of pendant leaf/bell drops; above that a band of small arched niches containing figures. Then a plain parapet and the low roof.

NORTH AND SOUTH FACES of the ardha-mandap: the same solid wall, with one small doorway on the centre line. MEASURED off the 1910 ASI plan: clear opening ~1.03 m at the outer wall face, widening to ~1.28 m within the 1.75 m wall thickness. Over it, projecting ~0.45–0.50 m, a hood ~2.1–2.3 m wide. Growse p. 254, exactly: the choir "is peculiar in having also, both north and south, a small doorway under a hood supported on eight closely-set brackets carved into the form of ELEPHANTS." Eight per door, two doors, sixteen brackets on the building. Opening:solid on these faces is about 1:40 (≈2.5%).

On the bracket pitch: Growse gives the count and the word "closely-set" and NO spacing. Eight brackets under a 2.1–2.3 m hood gives 0.27–0.31 m if the end brackets sit at the hood edges (width ÷ 7), or 0.26–0.29 m at an even pitch (width ÷ 8). The project's existing 0.28 m centres is therefore INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMED by the 1910 ASI plan. Keep it.

THE SHRINE EXTERIOR: completely blind. No windows, no niches with figures, no openings of any kind above the plinth — only the vertical roll mouldings, the horizontal rings, and the small square putlog holes. The whole octagon is a sealed drum.

ARCH GRAMMAR: hybrid Akbari/Jahangiri Braj. TRUE arcuate forms (the great arch, the cusped niche heads, the miniature arcade) sit directly over TRABEATE forms (the bracket-carried hood, the rectangular door architrave with its flat lintel). Do not make it consistently one or the other. There are no cusped multifoil "Mughal" arches on the main openings and no pillared open porch anywhere.

INTERIOR (relevant because a door is opened daily): the mandap's 1.75 m N and S walls are hollowed out with small chambers — Burgess calls them "closets in the side walls." A stair rises within the masonry on the south side of the shrine to a chamber INSIDE the sikhara. Growse cleared "from the upper room of the tower an accumulation of pigeons' dung more than four feet deep"; Amar Ujala, 17 Aug 2026, describes the stairs to the first floor and "मंदिर के शिखर के आंतरिक भाग" — the interior of the spire — both full of bats. Burgess also notes "the doors had all been arranged to slide back into slits provided in the walls" — sliding, not hinged.

## Material and colour

MATERIAL. Red sandstone ashlar, over a rubble/brick core. [SOURCED: Growse; ASI listing; Amar Ujala 2026 "लाल पत्थर से बने मंदिर"; Vrindavan Research Institute "spire made of red sandstone".] The core is visible wherever the facing has spalled in the 19th-century plates — so the wall is ashlar-FACED, not solid dressed stone, and the exposed patches are rough small-unit rubble with brick, a completely different texture from the dressed face. Quarry is INFERRED, not sourced: the Bharatpur–Dholpur–Rupbas red sandstone used throughout Braj. No paint, no lime wash, no gilding, no glazed tile, no plaster on the historic fabric — which is important because nearly every neighbouring building at Keshi Ghat IS lime-washed pink, ochre or white.

So: hue lives in a narrow band from about 350° to 22°; saturation 28–50%; value swings hugely with light, roughly V 29–33% in shade/haze and V 55–68% in direct sun. Build the material at roughly H 8°, S 40%, V 48% and let lighting do the rest.

TEXTURE — this is the part a modeller usually misses. The tower is NOT a uniform red. It is a visible CHEQUER of dark maroon-brown weathered blocks and scattered PALE BUFF/CREAM blocks, in no pattern, roughly one pale block in six, concentrated in horizontal streaks. These are old repairs and differentially weathered stone. Courses read clearly at distance: bed heights ESTIMATED at 0.35–0.50 m with long stretchers. The mouldings, the string courses and the amalaka ribs are much darker — near-black grime and biological growth collect in them, so the horizontal rings read as dark bands against a lighter field. The 1880s plate shows plants rooted in the mandap cornice.

## The setting

POSITION. OSM (ODbL) node 7288063975 "Храм Югалы Кишора" at 27.58698 N, 77.69869 E; the OSM Keshi Ghat node 6313899527 is at 27.58726 N, 77.69874 E — 32 m away. Independently, the ISVS precinct map (Sinha 2024, Fig. 3, key 7 = "Jugal Kishore Temple") places it immediately inland of the riverfront block at the south-west end of the Keshi Ghat kunj row, about 30–40 m back from the water. Both are approximate; treat as ±20–30 m.

IT IS NOT ON THE RIVERFRONT. This is the single biggest setting error available. A row of Jat-period riverfront kunjs stands between the temple and the Yamuna — Kishori Rani Kunj and Pandavali Kunj immediately in front, then Rani Lakshmi Kunj and Badan Singh Kunj along the ghat. Growse's own ghat list (p. 270) has, in order, 30 Kishor Rani's Ghat (built by Kishori, Rani of Suraj Mall of Bharatpur), 31 Pandawara Ghat, 32 Kesi Ghat (built by the Bharatpur Rani Lachhmi). Those kunjs are two- and three-storey havelis with arcaded riverfront facades, cusped arches, tapering columns, chhajjas, jaalis, bangladar roofs, domed chhatris, and octagonal burjes stepping into the water, with flights of steps between them (Sinha 2024, p. 47). The temple stands BEHIND them, one building deep, hemmed in on its other sides by dense two- and three-storey town fabric and narrow lanes. In the 1880s autotype a plain mud-brick/rubble building with small windows abuts it directly on the east.

VISIBILITY AT DISTANCE — only the sikhara, and essentially only from the river. In the 2015 river photograph and in Sinha's Fig. 1 (the iconic Keshi Ghat view from a boat), the Jugal Kishor tower is the ONE tall element on the whole skyline, rising clear above the kunj parapets; below the tower you can see only a sliver of the mandap's roof and cornice and one corner pilaster. From the town lanes it is invisible until you are effectively at its wall. Model the occlusion, or the landmark will read wrongly from every direction.

THE RIVER. Keshi Ghat is the only historic Vrindavan ghat whose steps still reach the water: "the historic ghats (with the exception of Keshi Ghat) no longer touching water" (Sinha 2024, endnote i, p. 52). The Yamuna turns south here, wrapping Vrindavan. The level is violently seasonal — the 2015 photograph shows water against the steps; a 2026 photograph at the same place shows a wide dry sand and silt bank with boats drawn up, stalls, and people walking out onto the silt. The far bank is low, flat, sandbarred and tree-fringed with no built skyline.

APPROACH. The Vrindavan parikrama marg runs along this stretch of ghat, so the crowd flows past the ghat, not to the temple. There is no axial approach, no processional street, no forecourt. You come off the parikrama path or the ghat into a lane and the temple presents itself as a wall at close range. Growse, p. 254, fixes it: it "stands at the lower end of the town near the Kesi Ghat." "Kesi Ghat" is one of Growse's named city quarters (mahallas, p. 271). Brajdiscovery says this temple "lies on a hillock near Keshi Ghat" — the 1880s photograph does show it standing on raised bare earth with the ground falling away on the flank, but no one has measured the rise.

ACTIVITY — none. This is a dead building, and the emptiness is a design requirement. The deities were carried away during Mughal raids and were never reinstalled, so the doors have been shut ever since (Amar Ujala, 17 Aug 2026). ASI's senior conservation assistant states that one staff member opens it each morning for cleaning and then locks it again. Bats roost on the internal stairs and inside the spire. The 300 m ASI regulated zone around this temple and Madan Mohan is what governs construction along the whole Keshi Ghat riverfront (Sinha 2024, endnote i). The living Jugal Kishore worship is elsewhere.

## What NOT to build

- Do not put the tower on the front block. The tall sikhara is over the REAR (west) shrine. The front block — the ardha-mandap, the one carrying the great arch and the elephant-bracket doorways — is FLAT-TOPPED: parapet, low stone slab roof, nothing above it. Burgess is explicit: 'Above the level of the mandap roof the sikhara tapers upwards.' This is the same inversion error the project already made at ISKCON.
- Do not add a kalasha, gold finial, metal pot, trident or flag. The crown is an amalaka — a ribbed flattened disc ~3.65 m across — with a low plain cap on it. Nothing above that in the 1880s autotype, the 1910 plate or the 2015 photograph.
- Do not make the sikhara an onion dome or a curvilinear latina shikhara. It is a steep, almost straight-sided CONE, only slightly convex in the lowest stage, crossed by exactly three string courses above the mandap roof. Growse himself argued its ancestor was the Buddhist stupa, not a Muslim dome.
- Do not model the shrine as an octagon with eight flat faces and eight sharp arrises. Burgess: 'octagonal in plan with the angles broken up so as to make it almost circular.' Eight broad flats plus a cluster of ~3 narrow roll mouldings at each of the eight corners. Use 24+ radial segments or the silhouette will be wrong from every angle.
- Do not build a nave, mandapa or porch in front of it. It is gone — Growse: 'The nave has been completely destroyed'; Burgess: 'the outer porch has entirely disappeared.' The 1910 ASI plan shows only a ~2.6 m platform-and-steps stub east of the wall. Two cells, nothing more.
- Do not give it a courtyard, compound wall or gateway. Growse describes gateways and courtyards for Madan Mohan and Gopinath; for Jugal Kishor he describes none, and the 1910 ASI plan shows no enclosure. It sits directly in the town fabric with buildings abutting it.
- Do not stand it on the riverfront or on the ghat steps. It is 30–40 m INLAND, behind the Kishori Rani and Pandavali kunjs. From the Yamuna you see the sikhara and a sliver of mandap roof and nothing else. From the town lanes you see nothing until you are at the wall.
- Do not populate it. It is empty and padlocked: the deities left during Mughal raids and were never reinstalled, ASI opens it each morning for cleaning and locks it again (Amar Ujala, 17 Aug 2026). No priest, no aarti, no bells, no garlands, no flower or prasad sellers at its door, no shoe rack, no queue, no loudspeakers, no cloth canopy, no electric light. Bats inside.
- Do not open the walls up. The building is roughly 95–97% solid. The east face has ONE doorway and four BLIND cusped niches; the north and south faces have one small doorway each; the shrine is entirely blind — no windows at all, only small square putlog holes. If your elevation reads as arcaded or airy, it is wrong.
- Do not treat the great east arch as an opening. Its head is filled with carved stone: a semicircular fan of pierced tracery, a central group of Krishna lifting Govardhan, four large disc rosettes, a small stupa-like finial. You should not be able to see sky or interior through it.
- Do not put the elephant brackets on the main east doorway. Growse places them on the SMALL north and south doorways — eight closely-set elephant brackets carrying a hood on each, sixteen in total. The east door has a rosette-studded rectangular architrave instead.
- Do not build it as solid dressed ashlar in good repair. It is ashlar facing over a rubble-and-brick core, and both 19th-century photographs show the facing lost over a large ragged area around and above the east arch, with the rough core exposed. (Whether ASI has since refaced it is unknown — but a pristine wall is certainly wrong for any pre-1950 state.)
- Do not paint the stone a bright uniform orange-red. It is a dark brownish-maroon in shade (H≈350–10°, S 37–50%, V 29–49%) lifting to a warm buff-red in sun (H 17–22°, S 28–34%, V 58–66%), and it is conspicuously PATCHY — roughly one pale buff or cream block in six scattered through a dark field, with near-black grime in every moulding.
- Do not hollow the mandap into one clean chamber. Its 1.75 m north and south walls contain small chambers ('closets in the side walls', Burgess), and a stair inside the masonry climbs to a chamber inside the sikhara — confirmed still there in 2026.
- Do not model the doors as hinged leaves. Burgess: 'the doors had all been arranged to slide back into slits provided in the walls.'
- Do not conflate this with the other two Jugal Kishors. The living Shri Yugal Kishor of the Nimbarka sampradaya is at Jugal Ghat, on the way from the Parikrama Marg to Radha Vallabh — a different building, in a different part of town. The Jugal Kishore temple at Panna (MP), built by Raja Hindupat Singh 1758–78, holds a deity said to have come from Vrindavan via Orchha. Neither is this ruin.
- Do not call it 'Akbar's temple' and date it 1570. Growse dates it Sambat 1684 = 1627 CE, in Jahangir's reign, founded by Non-Karan. Growse groups it with three earlier temples as a series 'commenced in honour of' Akbar's 1573 visit — which is 54 years earlier. The travel-site claim that it is 'one of four temples authorised by Akbar' compresses that.

## Uncertain — flag, do not assert

- HEIGHT IS THE WEAKEST FIGURE IN THIS BRIEF. No source — Growse, Burgess, ASI, press — gives any height. My 14.2 m for the sikhara above the mandap roof and ~22 m overall are photogrammetric, scaled from the 1910 plan onto two photographs. The sikhara figure I would trust to ±2 m because the 2015 view is long-lens and near-level; the wall-block height (8–10 m) is much weaker because its base is hidden behind buildings in every photograph I could find. Total could reasonably be 19–26 m.
- Growse's '25 feet square' choir versus Burgess's '17½ ft square inside' with 5 ft 9 in walls. The 1910 ASI plan agrees with Burgess (I measured its east wall at 5 ft 10 in). I have used Burgess. But Growse measured the building personally, had it cleared out himself, and gives 20 ft for Madan Mohan's choir by the same method — so his 25 ft may be an external dimension I am mis-reading. If you ever need the difference to matter, this is the crack.
- The Vrindavan Research Institute page states the temple is 'octagonal both externally and internally'. Burgess says the cella is SQUARE inside (~16 ft) and octagonal outside. The 1910 plan clearly shows a square interior. I have gone with Burgess, but a modern Indian source disagrees.
- No modern photograph of the EAST FRONT was found anywhere. Everything I say about the great arch, its carved tympanum, the rosette architrave and the blind niches comes from two photographs of one 1870s–80s negative. ASI has held the building for over a century and has certainly done some consolidation. The front may well look different now.
- The top of the amalaka. In the 1880s autotype it reads as an open, dished ring of lobes; in 2015 there is a smooth low cap on it. That may be an ASI repair, or may just be light and print quality. I cannot tell.
- Amalaka rib count (~18–20) is my count of 9–10 lobes across the visible half of a distant photograph. Low confidence. The 1880s plate, printed differently, suggests more.
- The corner treatment of the sikhara — I read about three narrow roll mouldings per corner from a distant photograph, giving roughly 24–32 facets in total. The exact number is a guess; the CHARACTER (clustered narrow rolls at the corners, broad flats between, near-circular silhouette) is certain.
- OSM's node for the temple is volunteer-placed and there is no geotagged photograph of the building itself. The 32 m separation from the Keshi Ghat node is consistent with the ISVS precinct map, but treat the coordinate as ±20–30 m.
- The 'hillock'. Brajdiscovery says the temple 'lies on a hillock near Keshi Ghat', and the 1880s photograph shows raised bare earth with the ground dropping on the flank. Nobody has measured the rise and I would not model more than a metre or two without better evidence.
- The founder. Growse gives 'Non-Karan', said to be a Chauhan Thakur, but adds 'it is not improbable that he was the elder brother of Raesil, who built the temple of Gopinath' — which would make him a Shekhawat Kachhwaha instead. Modern Hindi sources give नोनकरण / Nankaran / Naukaran. Clan and spelling are unresolved.
- The Panna connection (the deity being the one taken from here) is traditional and repeated everywhere, but Brajdiscovery records SEVERAL distinct Jugal Kishor deities in Vrindavan with different histories. The Amar Ujala report only says the images were sent away during a Mughal attack and never returned. I would not assert the Panna link as fact.
- Colour hexes come from ONE 2015 photograph under hazy light, uncalibrated, with no colour chart. The white-balanced values are my correction against the sky, which is an assumption. The sunlit reference values are from a DIFFERENT red-sandstone building nearby.
- Whether the ardha-mandap ever carried its own smaller tower that has since been lost. Growse records exactly that loss at Madan Mohan ('the upper part of the choir tower has also been destroyed') and Gopinath ('the three towers have been levelled with the roof') — but says nothing of the kind here, only that the nave went. The 1880s photograph shows a finished cornice, parapet and roof with no stump. I have built it flat-roofed on that evidence, not on a statement.
- The Growse edition. The scanned copy I quote (Bavarian State Library bsb11486712) has a title page reading 'THIRD EDITION, Revised and Abridged, 1886'. The third edition is normally cited as 1883. Page numbers should hold, but check if a page citation is being used formally.

## Independent check

Confidence: High on the documentary and architectural findings: I read Growse's third edition (1883) and Fergusson & Burgess vol. II p.158 as page images rather than OCR, downloaded the 1880s autotype and the 2015 photograph from their originating repositories, and read the 1910 ASI plan at higher resolution than the Commons crop the surveyor used. The headline claim you were most worried about - what crowns the building - survives: it is an amalaka with a low plain cap and no kalasha, and the tall tower is over the rear shrine while the front block is genuinely flat-topped in BOTH photographs. Medium on the numbers: my sikhara scale and the survey's both hang on the same plan-derived ~9.9 m diameter at the mandap-roof ring, so the heights are only as good as that one assumption, and the axial-steps and niche-size corrections are the ones I would most want a second pair of eyes on before they go into the model. Low on anything about the building's present state - no modern photograph of the east front exists, the only modern image of any kind is eleven years old, and ASI has held the building for over a century.

- NICHE SIZE IS ROUGHLY HALF THE TRUE SIZE. The survey gives the blind cusped niches flanking the east door as '~0.4 x 0.9 m'. Measured off my own crop of the Growse autotype (/private/tmp/claude-501/-Users-sushilbajpai-Desktop-Projects-livebraj/7fcafe1c-f37a-48dc-90d1-37908875b73c/scratchpad/g_front.png) against the doorway in the same frame, each niche recess is ~0.50x the door's width and ~0.58x its height. Using the survey's own door estimate (1.3-1.6 m x 2.8-3.3 m) that gives ~0.7-0.8 m wide x ~1.6-1.9 m tall. At 0.4 x 0.9 they will read as postage stamps on a 9 m face.
- THE SURVEY CONTRADICTS ITSELF ON THE STEPS, AND THE PLAN WINS. The elevation section says 'a flight of steps set on the FLANK ... rather than on axis'. I read Woodcut 353 from the archive.org page scan (b28711270_0002 leaf n199, ~750x570 of plan area) rather than the 728x428 Commons crop the surveyor used: east of the mandap wall the plan draws a stepped platform with a CENTRAL FLIGHT OF ~5-6 TREADS ON THE BUILDING'S AXIS, flanked by platform wings, plus a smaller flight on one flank. The flank steps in the photograph are real, but the axial flight is the primary approach and is in the very drawing the survey claims to have measured. This also softens 'There is no axial approach'. See /private/tmp/.../scratchpad/plan_stair.png and plan_hr.png.
- THE ROLL MOULDINGS SIT ABOVE THE NECK, NOT BELOW IT. The survey says the amalaka 'overhang[s] a short fluted neck that sits on a cluster of 3-4 horizontal roll mouldings'. Both photographs show, top-down: smooth cap, a small upper ribbed ring, the gadrooned amalaka, THEN a cluster of 3-4 thin roll mouldings, THEN a short plain neck, THEN a bold ring, then the cone. The mouldings are between amalaka and neck. See /private/tmp/.../scratchpad/amal.png.
- AMALAKA RIB COUNT IS TOO LOW. The survey says ~18-20 ribs in the round from '9-10 lobes across the front half'. I count ~13 lobes across the front of the 2015 photograph and ~14-15 scallops across the rim of the 1880s autotype, giving ~24-30 in the round. Model at 24+ ribs, not 18-20. (The survey's own uncertainty note concedes the plate 'suggests more' - that concession should have overridden the figure in the brief.)
- THE AMALAKA IS MUCH FLATTER THAN '~1.9 m TALL'. Using the survey's own calibration (D=9.9 m at the mandap-roof ring gives 1 px = 0.0202 m in File:Kesi Ghat - panoramio.jpg), the lobed ring spans only ~40 px vertically = ~0.8 m, plus a ~0.5 m cap above it. Diameter checks out at ~3.76 m against their 3.65 m, so the disc's proportion is roughly 4.5:1, not 1.9:1. Build it ~3.7 m across x ~0.8 m deep with a low cap, or it will read as a drum.
- THE LOWER DRUM DOES NOT TAPER. The survey's profile table has h=-4.6 m, D=9.3 m against 9.9 m at the roof ring, implying the base narrows. My right-edge silhouette scan of the 2015 photograph (axis at x=4015) gives a CONSTANT D of 474-480 px (~9.6 m) from h=-6 m up to h=+4 m, interrupted only by two projecting rings that reach ~495 px (~10.0 m). The lowest ten metres is a straight cylinder with projecting rings. Do not batter it inward at the bottom.
- 'INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMED BY THE 1910 ASI PLAN' IS FALSE FOR THE BRACKET PITCH. The plan draws the hood slab and two end supports only; it does not draw eight brackets (see /private/tmp/.../scratchpad/plan_door2.png). The 0.28 m centres are Growse's count of eight divided into a hood width measured off that same plan - arithmetic, not a second source. Keep 0.28 m if you like, but not on this justification. I also measure the hood at ~2.38 m, slightly wider than the stated 2.1-2.3 m.

File:Keshi Ghat, Vrindavan, Uttar Pradesh, India old temple 1951 - panoramio.jpg is categorised on Commons under 'Govind Dev Temple', is a consumer-camera JPEG of 18 Feb 2010 with a date stamp and scaffolding, and shows a visibly paler, pinker, better-preserved stone (S 19-34%, V 58-66%). (a) 'A stair rises within the masonry on the SOUTH side of the shrine' - the 1910 plan shows a stair in the shrine wall at the mandap junction on one flank, but the drawing carries no north arrow, so the side is not determinable from it, and neither Growse nor Burgess says. ### Independently confirmed

- WHAT CROWNS IT: AMALAKA, NO KALASHA - CONFIRMED, and this is the claim you asked me hardest to break. Burgess 1910 p.158 reads verbatim 'crowned by an amalasila' (I read the page image, not OCR). I downloaded File:Kesi Ghat - panoramio.jpg fresh from the Commons API and cropped the crown: a gadrooned, strongly flattened disc with a low smooth cap on top and nothing above it. The 1880s autotype, downloaded from the Bavarian State Library IIIF, shows the same ribbed ring with no finial. No kalasha, pot, trident, flag or gold in either photograph. The survey's own uncertainty about the 1880s 'open dished ring' resolves simply: that plate is a steep upward view, so you see the lobes' undersides; the 2015 view is near-level and shows the cap. Same object.
- TOWER OVER THE REAR SHRINE, FLAT-TOPPED FRONT BLOCK - CONFIRMED, and more strongly than the survey claims. It treats the flat roof as 1880s evidence only. In the 2015 river photograph the mandap block is visible left of the tower (camera GPS 27.588471/77.700119 puts it NE of the temple, so east projects to the left of frame): a red block with a bold projecting cornice, a parapet and a plainly flat roof, no stump, no chhatri. Burgess's sentence 'Above the level of the mandap roof the sikhara tapers upwards' is verbatim. The ISKCON-style inversion is not present here.
- EXACTLY THREE STRING COURSES ABOVE THE MANDAP ROOF - CONFIRMED by my own measurement, not by trusting Burgess. Scanning the tower silhouette in the 2015 photograph I find bold rings at y = 1180, 1305, 1475, 1680 and 1920; the mandap parapet sits at y ~1612, so three rings lie above roof level, one at roof level, one lower on the drum. Exactly the structure the survey's table asserts.
- THE SIKHARA PROFILE TABLE REPRODUCES. Calibrating on the survey's own D=9.9 m at the roof ring gives 1 px = 0.0202 m; their h/D pairs then come back within ~5-10% (h=+9.9/D=5.0 measures 10.1/4.85; h=+4.0/D=9.0 measures 4.1/9.49). ## Built — 2026-10-02 (JugalKishoreMandir.js)

What was here before: Growse's loose 25 ft cube with a tower on a guessed
profile, walk-in, turned the right way but 6 m off.

**Where.** An edge scan of ESRI z19 imagery (measurement only) finds the
building's south and north faces at world z -765.4 and -775.9 — 10.5 m, the
plan's 9.5 m octagon on its plinth — on an east-west axis, its masonry to
x ~819 and its steps to ~823. The origin is the centre of that masonry, 6 m
south-east of OSM node 7288063975. Frame: +lx north, +lz east, the front.

**Built to the 1910 ASI plan** (Fergusson & Burgess p.158): the ardha-mandap
17 ft 6 in square inside with 5 ft 9 in walls, flat-roofed behind a plain
parapet, nothing on it; the shrine octagon 9.5 m across the flats with its
angles cut back into rolls so it reads round; 18.1 m of masonry; the 2.6 m
platform stub where the porch was, with the checker's central flight of five
risers on the axis and a smaller one on the north flank; a stepped plinth.

**The sikhara**, the survey's profile with the checker's corrections: a
straight drum to 4 m over the mandap roof with two projecting rings (not
battered); three string courses; a fast shoulder to the neck; a bold ring,
the plain neck, three thin roll mouldings, then the flat gadrooned amalaka —
3.7 m across, 0.8 m deep, 26 ribs — a small ribbed ring and a low smooth cap,
and nothing above it. Putlog holes over the faces. About 23 m to the top.

**The east front:** the doorway in its rosette-studded architrave, shut (the
doors slide; they are locked), the checker's projecting hood with a saw-tooth
fringe, a blind arcade of seven over the lintel, two blind niches each side
at the checker's ~0.75 x 1.75 m; the great arch of voussoirs, near-semicircular
with a faint point, its head a fan of pierced tracery dark behind, the group of
Krishna lifting the hill above it, four disc rosettes, the small finial; and
the facing lost round the arch's head as both nineteenth-century photographs
show it (whether ASI has refaced it is unknown). North and south: the small
doors under hoods on eight elephant brackets each, shut, and the framed panels.
Colour: the checker's H 0-5, S ~40, V ~40.

**Closed.** "One staff member opens it each morning for cleaning and then
locks it again." So there is no walk-in interior: darshan is kept on the
platform at the door, the empty altar (altars.js: its lamps, tended) sealed
behind it. The builder marks the interior `closed`; `halls` and `temples`
leave closed temples out, and `kesi.mjs` walks what is true of this one: up
the flight to the door, stopped by it, kept off the plinth from the side.

**Not built, queued:** the closets in the mandap's side walls, the stair in
the shrine wall to the room inside the sikhara, the bats; the kunjs between
the temple and the river are the riverfront's, not this builder's.
