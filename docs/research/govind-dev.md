# Shri Govind Dev Ji Mandir, Vrindavan — exterior

*Researched 2026-09-27. Growse's full public-domain text (2nd and 3rd
editions), his own 1870s photographic plates and measured ground plan,
Fergusson/Burgess 1910 with Capt. Cole's plan, John Murray's c.1858
photograph, a 1949 government photograph, and modern photographs sampled for
colour. ASI monument N-UP-A195, Agra Circle.*

## Headline

**There IS a dome — Growse is emphatic that it survives intact — but it is
invisible from outside.** It is a Hindu vault over the crossing, cased
externally in a flat-topped, stepped, rectangular masonry block.

**Delete the onion dome and delete the gold finial.** There is no finial
anywhere on the main mass. The roofline is flat and stepped throughout.

## What survives — build only this

One immensely massive **flat-topped** building, read in three external tiers
(this is what "three storeys" in the guidebooks actually means):

1. **Plinth + lower stage** — deeply moulded plinth, a wide flight of steps at
   the east, then an arcaded lower storey of cusped-arched bays between giant
   stepped pier-masses.
2. **Upper stage / triforium** — an external open pillared gallery with
   slender colonnettes and deep bracketed eaves, running round the building.
   Growse: the walls are "pierced in two stages, the upper stage being a
   regular triforium, to which access is obtained by an internal staircase"
   (3rd ed. p.241).
3. **Attic / crossing block** — above the centre, a further rectangular block
   with a cusped-arched opening and a very heavy moulded cornice,
   **terminating flat**. A lightning conductor is the only thing sticking up.

The whole is a stack of horizontal cornices, string courses and saw-tooth
(chevron/dog-tooth) bands, with the corners "accentuated with singular force
and decision" (Fergusson) as giant stepped buttress-masses receding in tiers.
Fergusson: "It is, however, the combination of vertical with horizontal lines,
covering the whole surface, that forms the great merit of the design."

Growse's 1873–77 restoration "renewed" the "porches at the four corners of the
central dome" (p.247) — four small pillared elements at the corners of the
crossing block.

### The one vertical accent, and it is at the BACK

The **choir (jagmohan) tower**, standing west/behind the crossing over the
antarāla. Growse's own plate (facing p.246) shows it as a **square, stepped
pyramid of receding tiers, each tier edged with a saw-tooth course, with small
jharokha-like openings on the faces, TERMINATING FLAT, with small kalash-like
pinnacles at the corners of the top terrace.** Not a curvilinear nagara
shikhara and not a dome.

He restored it in 1873–77 "excepting only that the finial and a few stages of
stone-work immediately under it were not added; for they had entirely perished
and, in the absence of the original design, Sir John Strachey would not allow
me to replace them" (p.247). He regretted it — "the picturesque effect would
have been immensely enhanced by giving the tower the pyramidal finish intended
for it, instead of leaving it with its present stunted appearance" (p.248) —
but **it is still flat-topped today.**

## What was intended but never built

Growse pp.242–243:

> "It would seem that, according to the original design, there were to have
> been five towers; one over the central dome, and the other four covering
> respectively the choir, sacrarium, and two chapels. **The sacrarium has been
> utterly razed to the ground, the chapel towers were never completed, and
> that over the choir, though the most perfect, has still lost several of its
> upper stages.**"

> "**The loss of the towers and of the lofty arcaded parapet that surmounted
> the walls has terribly marred the effect of the exterior and given it a
> heavy stunted appearance**; while, as a further disfigurement, a plain
> masonry wall had been run along the top of the centre dome."

**Five towers intended, at most two ever finished.** And on the central one,
p.249: "**The tower over the central dome was also, as I conjecture, never
carried higher than we now see it**; but the open arcades, which crowned the
facade, though not a fragment of them now remains, were probably put up, as
the stones of the parapet still show the dents of the pillars."

p.256: "the central dome of the nave … **is perfect; but it is impossible to
determine from thence with any certainty what would have been the outline and
proportions of the tower that the architect proposed to raise over it.**"

Fergusson/Burgess 1910 p.156 agrees the top is unknowable: "externally it is
not clear how it was intended to be finished", and "the śikhara is gone,
having been destroyed along with the cella."

The lost **arcaded parapet** is the one element arguable as documented — dowel
dents survive in the parapet stones — but not a fragment remains and Growse
did not restore it. **Do not add it.**

## What is legend

- **"Seven storeys."** Devotional tradition, repeated by every travel site, UP
  Tourism and Hindi Wikipedia. **Growse never says it. Fergusson never says
  it.** Neither the 1590 foundation inscription nor any 19th-century survey
  mentions storeys. Growse's documented scheme is *five towers*, mostly
  unbuilt. Build three external tiers.
- **"110-foot shikhara."** Blogs and aggregators only. No primary source.
- **The Aurangzeb lamp story.** Growse reports it explicitly as hearsay, in a
  footnote from a correspondent. Modern retellings add "40 kg of ghee, visible
  from Agra" — no source.
- **The wall on the dome.** Growse is deliberately even-handed: "It is
  generally believed that this was built by Aurangzeb for the purpose of
  desecrating the temple, though it is also said to have been put up by the
  Hindus themselves to assist in some grand illumination." It is the tall
  blank block visible at the summit in Murray's c.1858 photograph, and it was
  **demolished in August 1873** as the first act of Growse's restoration. It
  must not be on a present-day model.
- **"Built by the gods overnight, left unfinished at sunrise."** Local legend
  explaining the truncation.

**What is solid:** the sanctum and its tower were destroyed and the image went
to Jaipur; the two side chapels were left unfinished by the *original*
builders, with "large blocks lying on the top of their walls, ready to be
placed in position … just as they were left … when the work for some
unexplained reason was suddenly interrupted" (p.249). Growse attributes that
to Man Singh's death, not Aurangzeb. **Do not conflate the two causes.**

## The plan — confirmed cruciform, with numbers

Growse 3rd ed. p.241: "**The body of the building is in the form of a Greek
cross, the nave being a hundred feet in length and the breadth across the
transepts the same.** The central compartment is surmounted by a dome of
singularly graceful proportions; and the four arms of the cross are roofed by
a waggon vault of pointed form, not, as is usual in Hindu architecture,
composed of overlapping brackets, but constructed of true radiating arches as
in our Gothic cathedrals."

From Growse's own measured plate (scale 40 ft = 1 inch, facing p.248):

| element | Growse (internal) | metric |
|---|---|---|
| east steps + narthex | 15 ft deep | 4.6 m |
| cruciform nave, N–S across transepts | 100 ft | 30.5 m |
| cruciform nave, E–W | 100 ft | 30.5 m |
| choir (antarāla / jagmohan) | 20 x 20 ft | 6.1 x 6.1 m |
| sacrarium (garbhagṛha) — destroyed | 20 x 20 ft | 6.1 x 6.1 m |
| two lateral chapels flanking it | 20 x 20 ft each | 6.1 x 6.1 m |
| **wall thickness** | "an average thickness of ten feet" | **3.05 m** |

Fergusson/Burgess 1910 p.156 gives the cross externally: "the porch measuring
**117 ft. east and west, by 105 ft. north and south** … Over the four arms of
the cross the vault is plain, and of **23½ ft. span**, but in the centre it
expands to **35 ft.**"

**Overall external envelope.** OSM's detailed satellite-derived trace (way
99427138, 161 nodes) measures **56.2 m E–W x 34.4 m N–S** = 184 x 113 ft,
independently confirming Growse's components, which sum to ~185 ft E–W.
**Use 56 m x 34–36 m.**

The trace also resolves the arms: each transept projects to ±17 m from centre
and is ~15 m wide; the east arm projects ~10.5 m and is 13–16 m wide; and the
**western sanctuary block is ~11.5 m deep x 33 m wide** — exactly choir +
sanctum + two flanking chapels + 10 ft walls. So **the side chapels still
stand** and the west end is nearly as wide as the transept span. **It is not a
narrow tail.**

Growse on Fergusson's plan (p.248): it shows the choir "in faint lines, as if
destroyed" when it is the best-preserved part, and "entirely omits the two
chapels that flank the cella on either side and are integral portions of the
design."

**Height: NO DOCUMENTED FIGURE EXISTS.** Not in Growse, not in Fergusson, not
published by the ASI. A photogrammetric estimate cross-checked against the
35 ft crossing span and 23½ ft arm spans gives **17–22 m to the top of the
crossing block**, ~20 m as a working value, with the flanking arm cornices at
roughly two-thirds of that, 12–14 m. **Estimated, not sourced.**

## The elevation

- **Plinth.** "Handsomely moulded", and it matters: in 1873 Growse removed
  debris "which had accumulated round the base of the building to the
  astonishing height of **eight feet and in some places even more, entirely
  concealing the handsomely moulded plinth**; a considerable increase was thus
  made to the elevation" (p.246). Fully exposed today, with a broad flight of
  ~6 steps and a landing at the east.
- **Pilasters.** Not thin pilasters — **giant stepped buttress-masses** at
  every external corner and re-entrant, each several receding rectangular
  tiers, each tier carrying its own cornice and saw-tooth band. The dominant
  vertical element.
- **String courses.** Very numerous. The wall reads as horizontal banding:
  plain ashlar → dentil/petal band → saw-tooth chevron band → projecting
  cornice slab, repeated.
- **Openings, lower stage.** Cusped arched bays between the piers, with carved
  brackets and colonnettes. Growse p.242: "**the arches are decorative only,
  not constructural: the spandrels in the head might be — and, as a fact, for
  the most part had been — struck out, leaving only the lintel supported on
  the straight jambs**, without any injury to the stability of the building."
  Cusped screens over a trabeated frame.
- **Openings, upper stage.** An external **open pillared gallery** with
  slender turned colonnettes and very deep bracketed eaves.
- **Clerestory.** Above the triforium, small pointed lights at vault level.
- **Jharokhas.** Projecting aedicular niches on carved brackets, flanked by
  colonnettes, with makara brackets, lotus rosettes and petal bands. Fergusson
  illustrates one as the exemplary detail of the whole building.
- **Chhajjas.** Continuous projecting eaves on carved brackets, with a row of
  small cylindrical pendant "peg" stubs beneath — a very characteristic detail.
- **Parapet.** **Plain and stepped today.**
- **East front.** Three-part: a tall central projecting bay (porch with two
  free-standing and two engaged columns under cusped arches, a deep saw-tooth
  chhajja, a large grilled opening above, a cusped-arched attic opening above
  that), flanked by lower recessed wings. Growse rebuilt the whole upper part
  in 1873–77, so it is partly Victorian fabric.
- **Effect of the vault on the exterior: NONE VISIBLE.** The pointed waggon
  vaults over the arms and the dome over the crossing are entirely concealed
  by flat stepped terraces and parapet slabs. **The single most important fact
  for the silhouette.**
- **Opening vs solid.** East front **~12–18% opening**, overwhelmingly solid;
  north and south transept ends **~25–30%** because of the external arcades.
  Growse's phrase for the whole is "so massive a building". **Model it as a
  fortress, not a pavilion.**
- **Sculpture.** It has "none of the coarse figure sculpture which detract so
  largely from the artistic appearance of most Hindu religious buildings."
  Ornament is architectural. Jacquemont (1829–30) noted "an immense number of
  bells, large and small, carved in relief on the supporting pillars."

## Material and colour

Red sandstone throughout. **Sources disagree on the quarry** — Jacquemont
(1829–30) says "from the neighbourhood of Jaypur"; Growse says the Bharatpur
quarries; modern scholarship says Tantpura, the same source as Fatehpur Sikri.
All three are the same Vindhyan geological family.

Sampled from modern photographs, mean RGB over ~20x20 px patches:

| condition | hex | HSV |
|---|---|---|
| bright sunlit dressed stone | `#B66741` | H19 S64 V71 |
| bright sunlit, typical | `#A25A3C` | H18 S63 V64 |
| sunlit plinth mouldings | `#A76A4D` | H19 S54 V65 |
| diffuse / mid-tone carved face | `#8E4B35` | H15 S63 V56 |
| in shade | `#6F3820`–`#572E1F` | H16–18 S64–71 V34–44 |
| bleached / exfoliated / spalled | `#E5B18F` | H24 S38 V90 |
| bleached to near-cream | `#F6DDC2` | H31 S21 V96 |
| soiled crevice / under cornice | ~`#0A0907` | near-black |

**Working base albedo ≈ `#A96A47`, H20 S58 V66**, with per-block variation
across H 15–27, S 35–70, V 20–75. The building is **not** uniform: strongly
patchy, mixing deep brick-red blocks with pale buff-cream ones, from (a)
1873–77 replacement stone, (b) spalling exposing fresh pale faces, (c)
differential weathering.

**Exterior: bare stone, no plaster, no paint.** The whitewash episode is
*interior*: in 1882 a Jaipur agent held a wedding party under the dome and
"thought nothing of whitewashing the walls and pillars of the interior up to
about half their height, thus ruining the architectural effect, which depends
so much on the rich glow of the red sand-stone" (p.245 fn). Modern interior
photographs still show pale patches at exactly that half-height — the removal
was incomplete. **Do not whitewash the exterior.**

**Weathering is DRY-climate. No lichen, no moss, no green growth.** Instead:
dark grey-to-black soiling in every re-entrant, under every cornice and in
every shadowed moulding; vertical dark streaks below projecting courses; pale
bleaching and spalling on sun-and-rain-exposed horizontals; a matte dusty
finish.

## The setting — the second big error

**"Standing alone in a large empty field" has been wrong for at least 400
years.**

- **c.1858** (Murray photograph): dense masonry houses, courtyards and
  terraces pressed right up against the temple on every side.
- **1873** (Growse p.246): "Many of the houses which had been allowed to crowd
  the courtyard **close up to the very walls of the temple** were taken down,
  and two broad approaches opened out … **Previously, the only access was by a
  narrow winding lane; and there was not a single point from which it was
  possible to obtain a complete view of the fabric.**"
- **Today:** an ASI-maintained open compound immediately around the building
  (paved apron and steps at the east, informal open ground) but **multi-storey
  town buildings stand hard against it** — three- and four-storey concrete
  directly visible through the east doorway, **overhead cables crossing the
  facade**, a low iron fence, an ASI blue signboard at the foot of the steps,
  and a street of shops. The approach is from Gopinath Bazar.

**The modern temple.** Growse p.249: "**A modern temple, under the old
dedication, has been erected within the precincts** and absorbs the whole of
the endowment." Dated c.1819 by secondary sources, it stands **immediately
behind (west of) the old building, on the site of the destroyed sanctum**, and
is reached through the choir doorway. The satellite trace shows the western
block as one continuous mass. **It is attached, not detached.**

**Rani Bhim's chhattri** (documented 1880s, present status unverified): Growse
dismantled a "large domed and pillared chhattri of very handsome and
harmonious design", inscribed Samvat 1693 (1636 AD), from the south side of
the choir and re-erected it "on the platform that marks the site of the old
sacrarium, where it serves to conceal the bare rubble wall that rises behind
it" (p.247). This is the only genuinely domed element on the site, and it is a
small ground-level pavilion at the *west*, not on the roof. Travel sites'
claim that the temple is "crowned with a majestic chhatri" is probably a
garbled version of this.

**Enclosure:** no published ASI figure found. Footprint 56 x 34 m; the cleared
compound is at most a few tens of metres deep and is bounded by the town. The
OSM "park" polygon nearby (85 x 126 m) lies 100–200 m to the south-east and is
a different space. **Keep-out should be tens of metres, not hundreds, with
dense low-rise town against it.** If a number is needed, an irregular compound
of roughly **70–90 m E–W x 50–60 m N–S**, flagged as estimated.

**The mound.** The temple stands on a raised mound (the Yogapīṭha), which is
why the east approach is a long flight of steps. Not a flat site.

## What NOT to build

1. **The onion dome.** No onion dome, ever, at any period.
2. **The gold finial / kalash on the main mass.** Nothing. The top is flat,
   with a modern lightning rod.
3. **Any tall shikhara over the crossing.** "Never carried higher than we now
   see it."
4. **A curvilinear nagara shikhara anywhere.** The one surviving tower is a
   stepped saw-tooth pyramid with a flat top.
5. **A finial on the choir tower.** Growse was explicitly forbidden from
   replacing it.
6. **Seven storeys, or four demolished storeys.**
7. **The arcaded parapet.** Not a fragment survives; never restored.
8. **The mosque/Īdgāh wall on the dome.** Correct for c.1670–1873 only.
   Demolished August 1873.
9. **Whitewash or plaster on the exterior.**
10. **Marble.** The "marble, silver and gold altar" in some sources describes
    the lost image's altar, not the building.
11. **Lichen, moss, green growth.** Wrong climate.
12. **Minarets.** Hindi sources translate Growse's "five towers" as "पांच
    मीनार". They are śikharas, not minarets.
13. **An empty field.**
14. **A detached modern temple at a distance.** It is attached at the west.
15. **A narrow tail at the west.** The western block is ~33 m wide.

## Uncertain — flag, do not assert

- **Height.** No documented figure. 17–22 m is an estimate.
- **Present state of the choir tower.** Documented by Growse's own 1870s plate
  after his restoration; **no modern photograph of the west/rear elevation was
  found** to confirm it still stands as restored, or whether the 1819 temple
  now obscures it. Verify before modelling it prominently.
- **Present state of Rani Bhim's chhattri.** Documented 1877, unverified today.
- **Quarry source.** Jaipur vs Bharatpur vs Tantpura.
- **Who built the wall on the dome.** Growse gives both possibilities.
- **Enclosure dimensions.** No published figure.
- **Whether the sanctum was destroyed by Aurangzeb or never finished.** Growse
  says the sanctum *was* razed but the chapel towers and probably the central
  tower were *never built*, most likely because Man Singh died. Fergusson:
  "either he left [it] unfinished at his death; or, as is related, the śikhara
  of it was thrown down by Aurangzib."

## Dating and authorship

Foundation inscription, on the exterior of the north-west chapel ~10 ft above
ground, translated by Growse p.244:

> "In the 34th year of the era inaugurated by the reign of the Emperor Akbar,
> Sri Maharaj Man Sinh Deva, son of Maharaj Bhagavan Das, of the family of
> Maharaj Prithiraj, founded, at the holy station of Brinda-ban, this temple
> of Gobind Deva. The head of the works, **Kalyan Das**, the Assistant
> Superintendent, **Manik Chand Chopar(?)**, the architect, **Gobind Das of
> Delhi**, the mason, **Gorakh Das**."

A second, mutilated tablet records the temple "built in sambat 1647, i.e.,
A.D. 1590", under the direction of the two Gurus, **Rūpa and Sanātana**. (The
3rd-edition OCR corrupts 1647 to "1047"; the 2nd edition reads it correctly.)

Restoration 1873–77 by F. S. Growse, total cost **Rs 38,365** against a PWD
estimate of Rs 1,32,857; Rs 5,000 of it from the Maharaja of Jaipur.
