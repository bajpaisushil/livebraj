# ashta-sakhi

*Researched and independently verified 2026-09-27. Confidence: partial.*

## oneLine

Shri Radha Ras Bihari Ashta Sakhi Mandir — a plot-filling carved pink-sandstone building on a lane close to Banke Bihari, where Radha and Krishna stand on a faceted white marble altar against a fan of real peacock feathers with all eight of Radha's sakhis beside Them, four to each side: ten murtis on one altar.

## location

CORRECTED, AND THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT FINDING IN THE REPORT.

The temple is in the dense lanes immediately around Shri Banke Bihari Mandir, Vrindavan, Mathura district, Uttar Pradesh. The temple's own "How to Reach" page says, in full: "2 minutes walk from Shri Bankebihari mandir." Braj Ras's own photograph caption reads "Asht Sakhi temple located near Banke Bihari Temple."

POSTAL ADDRESS as the temple gives it, verbatim from its own site: "Hari Om Shakti charitable trust, Shree Rasbihari Ashta Sakhi Mandir, Parikrama Marg, VIP Road, Near Shree Banke Bihari Mandir, Vrindavan, Mathura, Pin : 281124. Tel: +91 9917348499." Note "VIP Road" — the approach road that runs from Parikrama Marg down to Banke Bihari — which suggests the temple may sit on or just off that approach rather than deep in the Loi Bazar galis. The facade photographs show a very narrow lane, so this is not settled.

THE OTHER ACCOUNT'S COORDINATE FRAME IS WRONG BY ABOUT 830 m, AND WRONGLY ANCHORED.
It states: "Banke Bihari Mandir itself is unnamed in OSM; the untagged place_of_worship node 7292425230 sits at 27.58476 N, 77.69699 E, which matches every other account of its position." I queried that node today. It is not untagged and it is not Banke Bihari. Its tags are amenity=place_of_worship, religion=hindu, name:ru="Храм Шахджи" — that is SHAHJI TEMPLE, the spiral-columned temple beside Nidhivan, 137 m from Nidhivan.

The real Banke Bihari Mandir:
- OSM node 7172091726, name:ru "Храм Банкебихари": 27.57998 N, 77.69050 E
- English Wikipedia, Banke Bihari Temple: 27°34′47″N 77°41′26″E = 27.57976 N, 77.69051 E
The two agree to about 25 m. The other account's anchor is 847 m from this point, and the site it recommends (27.5845, 77.697) is 829 m from Banke Bihari — a ten-minute walk through the galis, not two minutes.

"Banki Bihari Bazar" is mapped as four ways running roughly 27.5722–27.5786 N, 77.689–77.691 E — the bazaar lanes approaching the temple from the south.

SO: place Ashta Sakhi Mandir within about 120–180 m of 27.57998 N, 77.69050 E — roughly 27.579–27.582 N, 77.689–77.692 E, in the block between Banke Bihari and Radha Vallabh, on or just off the VIP Road approach. Mark that as an approximation, not a survey. For a 1:1 reconstruction, place it by the lane and by its neighbours rather than by a number.

OSM DOES NOT CONTAIN THIS TEMPLE — confirmed, not assumed. An Overpass query over 27.560–27.605 N, 77.670–77.720 E for any name matching Sakhi, सखी, अष्ट, Rasbihari or Ras Bihari returns six elements, all of them "Banke Bihari" shops, a dhaba and bazaar ways. Nothing matches this temple. The old-town core is thinly mapped: only 47 places of worship are tagged in the whole 27.578–27.590 / 77.690–77.703 box, most of them by a Russian-language pilgrimage mapper.

THE ONE DISSENTING SOURCE, and a point the other account misses. Vrindavan Today (15 Nov 2021) places the temple "right opposite to Madanmohan Temple in Vrindavan". Madan Mohan stands on the Dwadashaditya hill by Kali Ghat, far to the north-west. I would not build to it — but note that this SAME article is the only source in existence for the 1886 / Hetampur founding. You cannot take its date and discard its location without saying so out loud.

CONTEXT THAT WILL CHANGE, AND IT NOW MATTERS MORE. The Shri Banke Bihari Corridor is being built on about 5.5 acres around Banke Bihari — 22,260 m², a compact block roughly 150 m on a side. Reporting (Deccan Herald, LawBeat, Tribune India) puts it at 285 houses and families plus around 200 shopkeepers, with a PIL before the Allahabad High Court over the demolition of the Kunj Galis and century-old temples, and rehabilitation planned at Rukmini Vihar and Sunrakh Bangar. No source I found names Ashta Sakhi Mandir as acquired or to be demolished. But on the CORRECTED position — 120–180 m from Banke Bihari rather than 830 m — this temple's lane is at or inside the corridor boundary, not comfortably outside it. The other account's conclusion that "the lane context may not match in a year" is right; its implied safety margin is not. Date your surroundings and expect them to go.

## scale

NO PUBLISHED DIMENSIONS EXIST. The temple's own site gives none, its Google Maps embed is a generic "q=vrindavan" search at zoom 14 with no pin, there is no OSM building footprint, and no measured drawing is online. Everything below is read off photographs. Treat every number as ±20–30% and correct it the moment anyone gets a survey.

References used for scale: the entrance door opening; the three seated women in the Braj Ras kirtan photograph; the 1.2 m ceiling fans and the ~1.0 m chrome railings; the bay count of the ground-hall arcade.

FOOTPRINT: about 16–22 m of street frontage by about 25–32 m deep. Roughly 450–700 m².
- Frontage: I have narrowed the other account's 20–25 m. The ground hall reads about 12–14 m clear internally; adding walls and the entrance bay gives 16–22 m. Their bay-count derivation is sound in method but the oblique photograph does not show the full frontage.
- Depth: the ground-hall arcade runs about eight bays a side at roughly 2.2–2.5 m, so 18–20 m of hall, plus the vestibule at the front and the raised platform and sanctum block behind. This remains the weakest figure in the report: no photograph shows the building from the side or rear.

HEIGHT:
- Street facade to parapet: about 13–15 m. Built up as door head ~5–5.5 m including the name band, lower carved register with the circular jali rosettes ~3.5–4 m, upper carved register ~3.5 m, cornice and parapet ~1.5 m.
- Spire above the roof slab: about 6–8 m of curvilinear cone plus about 1.5 m of amalaka, kalash, rod and finial.
- Total apex above the lane: about 20–24 m.

KEY INTERIOR DIMENSIONS:
- Ground-floor hall: about 12–14 m wide × 18–22 m long, side-aisle ceiling ~5.5–6 m, raised centre with the skylight strip ~7–8 m.
- Lotus pit: white marble kerb ~0.35 m high enclosing about 4 × 3 m; lotus sculpture ~2.5–3.5 m across.
- Steps at the far end: about seven to nine risers, ~1.3–1.6 m total rise, ~8–9 m wide. (The other account says six risers over 6 m; the high-resolution brochure crop shows more risers and a wider flight.)
- Peacock gate: about 3.5–4 m wide overall across its four leaves, ~3.5 m high; each dwarpala ~1.3–1.5 m on a low plinth.
- Nandi: about 1.2–1.5 m long on a low black stone plinth.
- Upper prayer hall: tree-jali arched windows about 4–4.5 m to the apex, scaled against the seated kirtan party; hall ceiling ~5.5–6 m.
- Sanctum arch: pointed head reaching about 5 m; silver door about 2.5 m wide, 3.5 m high including the fixed cusped silver head.
- Altar: faceted white marble apron about 3–3.5 m across; platform about 1 m above the hall floor; darshan railing bowing out about 1.5 m.
- Murtis: Krishna roughly 60–70 cm, Radha slightly less, the sakhis 40–60 cm descending outward. Unverified — no photograph gives a reliable scale reference at the altar.
- Tree-of-life group: seated figures roughly 1.4–1.6 m seated height (scaled against the ~1.0 m chrome rail — slightly larger than the other account's 1.3–1.5 m), on a stepped marble platform ~0.5 m high, with the copper-alloy tree spreading about 3–3.5 m. The alcove soffit is low — perhaps 3.5–4 m, not the 5 m the other account gives.

The one figure worth anchoring to is the door opening. The other account gives 2.4 × 4.5 m; the photographed leaf proportion is about 1 : 1.9, which is consistent, but the absolute size is a guess and 2.0–2.4 m wide by 3.6–4.5 m high is the honest range. Set it first and proportion the elevation from it.

## architecture

VERDICT ON THE OTHER ACCOUNT: the fabric description is unusually good and I verified almost all of it directly against the temple's own captioned photographs and Braj Ras's album. The LOCATION is badly wrong (see "location") and there are six concrete fabric errors, corrected below and flagged again in "doNotBuild". Everything below is what I could see or read in a named source.

WHAT IT IS: a tall, solid, plot-filling masonry building entered by a single street door, with an ordinary multi-storey concrete building hard against it (visible at the left edge of the Braj Ras exterior photograph). No courtyard, no forecourt, no compound wall, no gopuram. One spire on the roof, not visible from the lane. At least two internal levels; the street elevation reads as roughly three storeys of wall because the halls are double-height.

COLOUR — SETTLED. Two independent photographs taken in natural daylight (Braj Ras exterior; Braj Ras upper-hall kirtan shot) show a DUSTY PINK-GREY / rose-buff sandstone, low saturation, high value. The temple's own brochure shows the same stone reading hot orange-apricot — that is artificial light plus print cast, NOT the stone. Model the daylight colour; use the orange only as the look under the interior tungsten/LED wash. The temple's own caption says "pink sandstone" and the sandstone is unpainted.

THE STREET FACADE (Braj Ras photo "Asht Sakhi temple located near Banke Bihari Temple"; temple's own tour images 1 and 2)
A flat vertical wall of carved sandstone rising straight out of the lane to a parapet, with no projecting balcony anywhere. Shot from a lane too narrow to step back in: the camera is tilted steeply up and the top of the wall is out of frame.

1. THE DOOR. A rectangular opening in a WHITE MAKRANA MARBLE frame. The temple's own caption: "Made of hard wood with copper inlays. Around it is sparkling white marble intricately carved with floral patterns. The sheet white marble is from makrana, Rajasthan." The jambs and reveals carry a raised diamond-quilt grid with a lotus rosette at every intersection — a cushioned / pineapple-diaper texture, and it is unmistakable in the photograph. Over the opening the marble forms a shallow cusped arch of dense floral low relief with carved spandrels. The leaves are dark hardwood almost entirely faced in embossed brass/copper sheet, divided into about seven or eight horizontal bands by beaded metal strips and studded with rows of round bosses. A chrome handrail runs up the middle of the entrance steps. In the temple's photograph the leaves stand open.

2. THE TWO NAME BOARDS — CORRECTED. The other account stacks both inscriptions over the door. The photographs do not support that. The temple's own door photograph shows ONE thin pale band immediately above the marble cusped arch reading ॐ श्री राधा रास बिहारी अष्टसखी मंदिर ॐ (one श्री, not two) and nothing else below the carved registers. The Braj Ras exterior photograph shows a DIFFERENT bay of identical mouldings in which the large board श्री अष्टसखी मन्दिर — big black Devanagari in a carved stone frame with a cusped mini-arch and floral spandrels — sits at the bottom with its own thin ॐ band above it. Best reading: the facade carries at least two identically treated bays, one with the doorway and one carrying the large name board beside it. I cannot see both in one frame, so treat the stacking as unresolved; build both inscriptions, but do not pile them on top of each other over the single door. Two small floodlights project from the wall beside the thin band.

3. LOWER CARVED REGISTER: square panels, each holding a large circular jali rosette roughly a metre across — a pierced swirling-arabesque medallion, genuinely open (black shows through). Separated by narrow carved pilasters with bases and capitals.

4. A carved string course of dense floral creeper running the full width.

5. UPPER CARVED REGISTER: large rectangular fields of genuinely pierced fine diagonal-mesh jali, flanked by narrow panels of carved relief that INCLUDE SMALL FIGURAL AND ANIMAL SCENES (clearly visible in the temple's own tour image 2 — the other account records only floral rosettes here). Plus square panels with a carved rosette centre and pierced corners.

6. One FALSE JHAROKHA centred over the entrance bay — a carved miniature balcony window with a small cusped canopy over a jali-filled opening, in relief only. The temple's own caption says so outright: "The facade has false jharokas (balconies)". Nobody stands on the front of this building.

7. A projecting carved cornice on brackets, then a low carved parapet with small pot finials.

The temple's own caption adds a point the other account underplays: "The lattice work lets in sunlight into the interior in interesting dappled patterns." The facade jali is functional, not decorative.

THE ROOF (temple's own brochure, two photographs, one full-strength)
ONE SPIRE, over the sanctum, set back so it is invisible from the lane. Its form: a tapering, near-circular, RENDERED AND PAINTED spire — but NOT a pure smooth cone. The profile is curvilinear, convex at the shoulder and drawing in to a point, closer to a bell or parabola. About twelve to sixteen vertical ribs and four to six horizontal bands, all picked out in DEEP MAROON-RED against a plain cream-buff ground. Small square PIERCED JALI VENTS are set into the spire face (at least two are visible) — the other account misses these. It springs directly from behind a low parapet of plain recessed panels; no cornice-ringed drum is visible, so do not build one. On top: a ribbed amalaka-like melon member, a small maroon ribbed pot, a stack of gold disc/sphere members on a rod, then a thin finial. A separate slender metal pole (flagstaff or lightning conductor) rises from the roof beside it.

Beside the spire, a stepped triangular parapet screen / false gable in the same pale stone, with a moulded coping, plain recessed panels, one large oval cartouche and a smaller roundel, and small domed kalash finials at the steps and ends. There appear to be two such gables at different heights. A deep flat chhajja on ornately carved brackets runs below the parapet along the roof edge. At roof level the masonry reads CREAM-BUFF, paler than the street front.

INSIDE — THE ENTRANCE (temple's tour image 3, photographed through the open street door)
A short vestibule: ochre-yellow walls, flat ceiling with recessed downlights and an amber glass chandelier, pale stone floor, a carved dark-bronze inner door frame. Straight ahead, a second studded wooden door with a small dark Hindi signboard above it. The temple's own caption: "You enter the main mandir and are faced with a doorway that is the entrance to the community hall. Flanking the door to the hall are two sets of stairs that lead to the upper floor and the main temple area." No photograph shows the stairs.

THE GROUND-FLOOR HALL — large, rectangular, verified in two photographs
- Long side walls: an arcade of round-headed arched recesses, about eight bays a side, the recesses painted MUSTARD-OCHRE YELLOW, piers and arch faces cream-buff with moulded bases and capitals. Each recess holds one large DARK-framed panel — pietra-dura marble inlay pictures on dark grounds, or big coloured high-relief sculpted murals (nauka vihar with a peacock-prowed boat on lotus water is confirmed in the brochure; also the jhula, the maha-raas circle, Govardhan). Beside each panel, a small dark Hindi plaque — Braj Ras captions one "Leela written on the wall".
- Ceiling: flat white coffered soffit in square recesses with downlights, and a raised central strip carrying a long DARK-GLAZED BARREL-VAULTED skylight clerestory. Long-drop ceiling fans.
- Floor: white marble with terracotta-orange marble in a bold interlocking stepped/zigzag pattern. Striking, and confirmed twice.
- Centre of the floor: a low white marble kerb enclosing a rectangular pit holding a large upright LOTUS sculpture — outer petals dark bronze-brown (not copper-gold), inner petals pink-red.
- THINGS THE OTHER ACCOUNT MISSES ENTIRELY, and they dominate the temple's own photograph of this hall:
  (a) A large bronze/brass NANDI — a couchant bull, richly caparisoned with a bell-collar and beaded necklaces, red tilak, painted eyes and lip, garlanded with marigolds, on a black stone plinth, standing in the near right foreground. Roughly 1.2–1.5 m long.
  (b) On the right wall, a projecting glazed shrine — a glass-fronted white marble cabinet in a carved sandstone frame with a floral carved lintel, hung with marigold and dark-green garlands, containing a seated saffron-clad murti (the face reads as Hanuman). Above it a violet-lit arched vault.
  (c) Beside that shrine, a large black tablet in a wooden frame carrying the MADHURASHTAKAM in white Devanagari, headed ॥ श्री मधुराष्टकम् ॥ — legible in the brochure at 300 dpi.
- FAR END: a broad flight of about seven to nine wide steps spanning some 8–9 m rising to a raised platform. At the head of the steps, a wall of carved orange-buff panelling with a central recessed shrine: a dark carved tympanum bearing a large ॐ in relief over an arched opening, within which a pale blue scale-patterned (peacock-feather) backdrop and a seated white deity group on a gold seat, flanked by two small carved dwarpala figures on brackets.
- To one side of the steps, a low table for a pujari and a microphone stand.

THE PEACOCK GATE (temple's tour image 8)
Four leaves of BLACK ironwork: two wide central leaves and two narrower side leaves. Each central leaf carries one large GOLD PEACOCK in profile facing inward toward its partner, crest up, long neck, and a huge cascading fan of overlapping gold tail feathers filling the lower two-thirds. The ground is a dense black arabesque grille with gold-tipped curls; the side leaves carry the grille without peacocks. The gate sits in a pale stone frame with carved corner brackets. Flanking it, one on each side on a low plinth, a DWARPALA carved in high relief on the pier face — turbaned, moustached, bare-torsoed, holding a mace upright, painted in part (yellow-gold turban with a jewel, red lips, green and gold detail on belt and mace), about 1.3–1.5 m. The temple's caption: "The exquisitely designed metal gate to the temple – with the motif of the peacock in full golden splendor."
WHERE IT STANDS IS NOT PROVEN. The other account places it at the head of the ground-hall steps. That is plausible — the shrine at the head of those steps is flanked by two dwarpala figures of the same type — but the only photograph of that head-of-steps shows an open shrine niche, not a gate. Build it there, and know the placement is inferred.

INSIDE — THE UPPER PRAYER HALL AND SANCTUM (brochure; Braj Ras kirtan photograph; tour images 4, 6, 7, 9)
- Walls: dusty pink-buff carved sandstone throughout, with carved pilasters, capitals, cornice bands, and carved sandstone plaques — one reads श्री राधा रास बिहारी अष्टसखी कुंज with a carved white lotus below the text, another carries a Hindi verse.
- SIDE WALLS pierced by tall pointed/ogee arched WINDOWS filled with WHITE MARBLE OPENWORK CARVED AS A TREE: trunk rising from the sill, branches and foliage filling the arch, with flowers INLAID in coloured semi-precious stone — lapis blue, jade green, coral red, cream. The temple's own site says "Semi precious stones imported from all corners of the world are used in inlay as well as in the murals." Below and flanking each arch, tall dark RED-BROWN wooden jali panels in a geometric star fret. Daylight pours through and throws dappled patterns across the floor. This is the single most beautiful thing in the building and the other account is exactly right about it.
- Ceiling: flat white plaster with cove lighting and, in the centre, a large ROUND RECESSED PANEL PAINTED DEEP BLUE — and it is a NIGHT SKY, with small pale stars picked out on the blue. The other account records the blue and misses the stars. Two long-drop ceiling fans.
- Above the sanctum's flanking niches, two PANELLED TIMBER LOUVRED SHUTTERS set high in carved sandstone frames, with small cusped carved relief tympana above them. Omitted by the other account.
- Floor: plain pale grey-white marble, with a long runner mat laid down the middle where the kirtan party sits, with a microphone stand.
- Strings of coloured bunting garland across the tops of the walls; a framed Radha-Krishna picture hung high between the windows; black loudspeaker boxes mounted high.

THE SANCTUM FRONT
A tall carved sandstone arch — deeply moulded, pointed with a small cusped/ogee apex, near-mihrab in profile — set in a rectangular frame with heavily carved floral spandrels and a carved apex motif, flanked by engaged round-shafted colonnettes with foliate capitals on tall bases.
Within it, THE SILVER DOOR. The temple's caption: "The ornate silver and metal coated door to the sanctum sanctorum — the door has intricate carvings that depict hindu symbols such as AUM and the kalash, the swastika, the conch shell and the peacock feather. Right at the Center top is the resplendent symbol of the sun." The cusped silver arch head with the circular SUN MEDALLION — and the sun has a FACE — is FIXED; the leaves below it OPEN for darshan (tour image 9 shows them open and the altar visible straight through). Below the sun: two large ॐ in the top row, then roughly four columns by three rows of square embossed panels carrying kalash, swastika, conch, peacock feather and lotus; narrow outer vertical panels with standing figures.
To left and right of the sandstone frame, two small cusped niches each holding a PAINTED PANEL on a bright blue ground — a standing sakhi in yellow-gold and turquoise holding a fan. Black loudspeakers on the piers. A semicircular stainless-steel tubular darshan railing on chrome posts bows out from the door across the marble floor.
Inside the doorway, a WHITE MARBLE arched surround carrying a row of about five round medallions along its head — a central ॐ alternating with dark floral discs — and a dark bell or hanging lamp on a chain from the arch. Omitted by the other account.

THE ALTAR
White marble, stepped and FACETED — the front apron is a polygonal/octagonal marble platform, not a rectangle, with a swastika marked at the centre front. A moulded intermediate ledge carries silver and gold vessels and lamps, and in one photograph three large arched silver-and-blue ornaments. Then the deity tier, then the raised centre where Radha and Krishna stand under a small ornate canopied singhasan. Small subsidiary murtis stand or sit on the front apron — in one photograph two small seated acharya figures reading from wooden book-stands, in another two small standing white figures, plus two small dark peacock figures lying on the marble.
Behind everything, filling the white marble arched niche, a SEMICIRCULAR FAN OF REAL PEACOCK FEATHERS in concentric rows — dark blue-green eye-spots radiating from behind the Deities — with a WHITE feather chhatra behind the central pair. Two small painted kalash motifs in the marble spandrels above the arch. A dark red darshan curtain hangs at the side.

THE TREE-OF-LIFE GROUP — PLACEMENT CORRECTED
A seated Radha and Krishna carved from a single block of pale pink sandstone, with painted faces and small inlaid coloured gems, Krishna playing the flute, on a stepped white marble platform, with a carved pink-stone PEACOCK lying on the platform beside Them (the other account omits the peacock). Above and behind Them a free-standing DARK COPPER-ALLOY TREE with gold-tipped metal leaves and, per the temple's own caption, "birds sitting on its branches". Chrome tubular railing on posts in a curved sweep; in one view a carved cream fretwork balustrade below the rail. Pale cream plastered walls with small square pierced cream-stone jali panels set into them, washed with violet LED; two CCTV cameras. The other account calls the walls ochre — that is the yellow lighting in the brochure shot; in Braj Ras's daylight-balanced shot they are pale cream.
The other account places this group "off the ground-floor hall in its own balustraded alcove". The temple's OWN caption places it at the far end of the MAIN PRAYER HALL: "The main prayer hall, facing the sanctum sanctorum on the cameraman's side. On the far end is a intricately carved sculpture of Radha Krishna against the backdrop of the tree of life." But the photograph that caption is attached to is unmistakably the ochre-arcade, orange-floor, lotus-in-the-pit hall — the same room the other account calls the community hall. The temple's own two captions therefore contradict each other about which room is "the main prayer hall". Unresolved. Build the group in a shallow recess with a flat soffit; do not commit hard to which floor.

MATERIALS, for a modeller
- Exterior and upper-hall walls: dusty pink-buff carved sandstone, heavily pierced with jali
- Entrance surround: white Makrana marble, diamond quilt with lotus rosettes
- Main door leaves: hardwood faced in embossed brass/copper with rows of bosses
- Upper-hall windows: white marble openwork with coloured semi-precious stone inlay; dark red-brown wooden star-fret jali below
- Sanctum door: silver-clad sheet metal (the temple says "silver and metal coated", so plating, not solid repoussé)
- Inner gate: black iron with applied gold peacocks and scrollwork
- Ground-hall walls: plastered and painted ochre-yellow with cream-buff arches and stone-clad piers
- Ground-hall floor: orange and white marble; upper-hall floor: plain grey-white marble
- Spire: rendered and painted, cream-buff with maroon ribs and bands, with pierced jali vents
- Altar deities: Krishna carved in BLACK STONE; Radha cast in a METAL ALLOY (temple's own caption, tour image 11) — the other account never says what the murtis are made of
- Lighting is entirely modern: recessed downlights, LED cove and wall-wash in blue and violet, and the temple states fibre-optic lighting is used in the interior

Deity garments are changed every day of the year.

WHERE THE RECORD IS THIN — unchanged and still true
- No plan, no section, no measured drawing, no published dimension of any kind. Everything dimensional is estimated off photographs.
- No photograph shows the building from the side or rear at ground level. Depth and the side and rear elevations are completely undocumented.
- No photograph shows a staircase. The vertical arrangement rests on one sentence in the temple's own tour caption. The stair geometry is invented.
- The two halls are unmistakably different rooms, but the temple's own captions disagree about which one is "the main prayer hall", and no source states how many floors the building has.
- The peacock gate's position in the building is inferred, not photographed.

## distinguishing

1. THE PEACOCK-FEATHER ALTAR WITH TEN FIGURES IN A STEPPED ARC. A fan of real peacock feathers in concentric rows fills the white marble arch behind the Deities, with a white feather chhatra behind the central pair. Radha and Krishna at the centre; four sakhis descending to the left and four to the right, each step outward one tier lower and one size larger, so the group reads as a shallow open fan. The front apron is a faceted white marble polygon with small subsidiary murtis and two small dark peacocks laid on it. Krishna is black stone; Radha is cast metal. Ten murtis, not nine — the other account's arithmetic slipped.

2. THE WHITE MARBLE TREE-JALI WINDOWS. Tall ogee-arched openings in the upper hall's side walls filled with white marble openwork carved as a TREE — trunk at the sill, branches filling the arch — with flowers inlaid in lapis blue, jade green and coral red, backlit by daylight, throwing moving dappled patterns across the grey marble floor, with tall dark red-brown wooden star-fret jali panels below and flanking. Verified in two independent photographs. If a modeller gets only one interior right, get this.

3. THE BLACK-AND-GOLD PEACOCK GATE. Four leaves of black iron and gold arabesque; the two central leaves each carry one large gold peacock facing its partner, tails cascading to the foot of the leaf; flanked by two part-painted sandstone dwarpalas with yellow-gold turbans and maces.

4. THE GROUND HALL: A BRONZE NANDI, A LOTUS IN AN ORANGE FLOOR. A bold interlocking stepped pattern of orange and white marble underfoot; a big bronze-and-pink lotus in a low white-marble-kerbed pit; a garlanded bronze Nandi in the foreground; ochre-yellow arcaded walls hung with dark-framed pietra dura and coloured high-relief murals; the Madhurashtakam on a black tablet; a barrel-vaulted dark skylight down the centre of a white coffered ceiling; a broad flight of steps at the far end to a shrine under a carved ॐ. It looks nothing like a temple mandapa and everything like a 2000s civic hall, which is what it is.

5. THE DOOR. Hardwood leaves faced in embossed brass, studded with bosses in seven or eight bands, in a white Makrana marble frame carved all over with a raised diamond quilt with a lotus at every crossing, under Devanagari name boards, at the foot of three storeys of flat carved pink-grey sandstone in a lane too narrow to step back in.

## doNotBuild

Corrections to the other account first — these are the ones that will actually bite.

A. DO NOT PLACE IT WHERE THE OTHER ACCOUNT PLACES IT. Its whole coordinate frame is anchored to OSM node 7292425230 at 27.58476 N, 77.69699 E, which it calls "Banke Bihari Mandir itself, unnamed in OSM". That node is NOT untagged and is NOT Banke Bihari: it carries name:ru = "Храм Шахджи" — SHAHJI TEMPLE, 137 m from Nidhivan. The real Banke Bihari is OSM node 7172091726 / Wikipedia 27.57976 N, 77.69051 E — 847 m away. The other account's suggested site (27.5845, 77.697) is 829 m from Banke Bihari, a ten-minute walk, and sits next to Shahji and Nidhivan. Building there puts the temple in the wrong quarter of the town. This is precisely the "Goswami temples get mixed up" trap.

B. DO NOT PUT TEN FIGURES ON THE ALTAR AND CALL THEM NINE. Radha + Krishna + four sakhis each side = ten murtis.

C. DO NOT LOCK THE SAKHIS' CLOTHES. The other account states every sakhi wears an orange-red veil over a gold crown above a turquoise skirt. That is one day's dressing. The garments are changed every day of the year and I have two photographs showing entirely different colour schemes.

D. DO NOT LEAVE OUT THE NANDI, THE HANUMAN SHRINE AND THE MADHURASHTAKAM TABLET. The bronze Nandi is the most prominent object in the foreground of the temple's own photograph of the ground hall and the other account does not mention it at all.

E. DO NOT STACK BOTH NAME BOARDS OVER THE DOOR. The large श्री अष्टसखी मन्दिर board appears to occupy a separate, identically moulded bay beside the entrance bay, with its own thin ॐ band above it.

F. DO NOT BUILD THE SPIRE AS A PURE SMOOTH CONE ON A DRUM. It is a curvilinear, convex-shouldered, bell/parabolic spire springing straight from behind a panelled parapet, with small pierced jali vents in its face, and no visible drum.

And the ones the other account got right, which still need saying:

1. DO NOT put the main altar at ground level facing the street door. The temple's own tour says the stairs beside the community-hall door lead up "to the upper floor and the main temple area." The room you walk into is not the shrine.

2. DO NOT build a courtyard temple. No courtyard, no compound wall, no forecourt, no plaza, no flight of steps up from open ground. A solid building filling its plot with one door onto a narrow lane and an ordinary multi-storey concrete building hard against it on at least one side.

3. DO NOT make it RED sandstone. Dusty pink-grey in daylight. The hot orange in the temple's brochure is interior lighting plus print cast, not the stone. Keep the value high and the saturation low.

4. DO NOT give it a latina shikhara, a Nagara spire with urushringas, or a dome. Rendered and painted cream with maroon ribs and bands, amalaka, kalash, finial. It reads more Bengali-ratna than Rajasthani — which sits oddly well with the single claim that a Bengali patron founded it.

5. DO NOT make the spire visible from the street. Set back over the sanctum, seen only from roof level. From the lane you see a flat carved wall and nothing above it. A render with the spire rising behind the entrance is the Chandrodaya mistake in miniature.

6. DO NOT model the jharokhas as balconies. The temple's own caption says "false jharokas". Nobody stands on the front of this building.

7. DO NOT carve the peacock gate from stone or make it a wooden door. Black painted iron with applied gold peacocks and scrollwork. The only stone at that gate is the two dwarpalas.

8. DO NOT collapse the two trees into one. Downstairs-or-far-end: a free-standing DARK COPPER-ALLOY tree with gold-tipped leaves and metal birds arching over a seated pink sandstone Radha-Krishna. Upstairs: WHITE MARBLE OPENWORK trees filling the arched windows, inlaid with coloured stone. Neither is a painted mural.

9. DO NOT paint the altar backdrop. Real peacock feathers in concentric rows plus a white feather chhatra. It must read as feathers, with the eye-spots catching light.

10. DO NOT line the sakhis up flat in a row of equal heights. Stepped arc: centre highest and smallest, outermost lowest and largest.

11. DO NOT make the interior all stone. The ground hall is plastered and painted ochre-yellow with cream arches, a white coffered ceiling with a dark barrel skylight, and an orange-and-white marble floor. Only the upper hall is sandstone-faced, and its ceiling is white plaster with a deep BLUE painted round medallion — a NIGHT SKY WITH STARS, not plain blue.

12. DO NOT date the fabric to 1886. Whatever its origin, what stands is a recent reconstruction and refit. Vrindavan Today says the temple fell into such disrepair that it "had to be sold" and was then restored; the temple's own brochure says "An ancient temple, it was in great dis-repair". The lighting is LED and fibre-optic, the railings are stainless steel, there is CCTV on the walls, and the temple's own site is copyright 2012. Model a modern building in traditional dress.

13. DO NOT place it opposite Madan Mohan. One source says so — Vrindavan Today, 15 Nov 2021 — and that same article is the sole source for the 1886 founding. The temple's own "How to Reach" page says "2 minutes walk from Shri Bankebihari mandir", and Braj Ras's own photo caption reads "Asht Sakhi temple located near Banke Bihari Temple". Put it in the Banke Bihari block — the REAL one, at 27.5798 N, 77.6905 E.

14. DO NOT confuse it with the Ashta Sakhi temple at BARSANA — which is real and older: Growse's "Mathura: A District Memoir" (3rd ed., 1883) describes it at the foot of the Barsana hill below the Ladli Ji temple, "dedicated to Radha's female companions, called the Sakhis, who are eight in number". Nor with the eight individual sakhi temples in their home villages. Correcting the other account's list against Wikipedia: Lalita at Unchagaon, Vishakha at Kamai, Champaklata at Karhala, Chitra at Chiksauli, Tungavidya at Dabhala, Indulekha at Anjanoka, Rangadevi at Rakholi, and SUDEVI AT SUNHERA — not at Rakholi as the other account has it.

15. DO NOT treat the corridor works as distant. See "location": on the corrected position the temple sits at or inside the edge of the Banke Bihari corridor footprint, not 800 m clear of it.

And one thing not to leave out: the lane. The temple's own and Braj Ras's photographs are both taken steeply upward from a few metres away, with a modern concrete block hard against the corner. Give it a clear site and you have built a different thing.

## deities

On the altar, ten murtis in a stepped arc, not nine. At the centre Shri Radha and Shri Krishna — the temple's own caption for the close-up: "Lord Krishna is depicted as Dark and the statue carved out of a special black stone that is rare. The statue of Radha Rani is made of an alloy of metals." Krishna stands in a pronounced swaying tribhanga playing the flute under a large jewelled radiating crown; Radha stands beside Him.

Flanking Them, four sakhis to each side, named by the temple as: Lalita, Campakalata (Champaklata), Visakha, Citra, Tungavidya, Indulekha, Rangadevi, Sudevi. The temple's own site says Lalita is "the main sakhi" and Visakha "the second most important", and gives each a traditional complexion and garment colour — Lalita bright yellow with peacock-feather-coloured clothes, Champaklata champaka-yellow, Visakha star-patterned garments, Citra saffron with crystal-coloured garments, Tungavidya saffron, Indulekha tan with pomegranate-coloured garments, Rangadevi lotus-filament with rose-coloured garments, Sudevi golden with coral-coloured clothes.

DO NOT hardcode the dresses. The temple changes the Deities' garments every day of the year, and the two photographs I compared show the sakhis in turquoise with orange-red veils in one and in green and white with red-pink veils in the other. Model the garments as swappable.

Also enshrined in the building, and absent from the other account: a large bronze Nandi in the ground-floor hall, and a glazed marble shrine on that hall's right wall holding a seated saffron-clad murti (reads as Hanuman). Separately, a seated pink-sandstone Radha-Krishna group beneath a copper-alloy tree of life, with a carved stone peacock beside Them.

The temple's claim — from its own site — is that it "is the first Indian temple that is dedicated to the divine couple and their Ashta Sakhi's". The defensible version for a caption is: unique in Vrindavan for placing all eight sakhis on the altar as murtis beside Radha and Krishna. "Only temple anywhere" is too strong — Growse recorded a temple of the eight sakhis at the foot of the Barsana hill in 1883, and each sakhi has her own village temple.
