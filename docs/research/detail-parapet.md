# Braj architectural detail: parapet

*Researched and verified 2026-09-27. Impact: transforms-it.*

## whatItIs

THE PARAPET AND ROOFLINE — what a Braj silhouette is actually made of.

Braj rooflines are flat-terrace rooflines. Outside the five 16th-century Akbar-period temples (Govind Dev, Madan Mohan, Jugal Kishor, Gopinath, Radha Vallabh), almost nothing in Vrindavan has a pitched or tower-dominated roof. Sinha & Dhariwal (ISVS, 2024) state the governing fact for the post-Aurangzeb haveli-temple type that most of Vrindavan is: "While no domes or soaring shikhar towers drew attention to the building, the entry gate known as gokhe is emphasized by porches on either side of the main door and the jharokhas (balconies) above." So the vertical drama is at the GATE, and everywhere else the building stops at a horizontal line. That line is the parapet, and at the moment your buildings do not have one that works — which is precisely why they photograph as boxes.

The vocabulary, in the order it stacks from the top of the wall:

1. CHHAJJA (already in the codebase). Growse's own glossary defines it: "Chhajja, stone eaves of a house or other building, supported on projecting brackets." At Rani Lachhmi's kunj on Keshi Ghat he records "unusually broad eaves which have a wavy pattern on their under-surface and are supported on bold brackets." The parapet stands BEHIND this, set back — never flush.

2. BED MOULDING / KERB. A single recessed dark course between the eave and the parapet wall. Two triangles' worth of tone does the work of a carved moulding: it is the shadow line that says the parapet is a separate thing sitting on the roof, not the wall continuing upward.

3. THE DWARF WALL (mundera). A solid 0.22–0.26 m thick low wall, roughly half a metre high, on the roof edge. This alone — with no merlons at all — is correct for a great many Braj buildings and is what most of your ghat and lane structures should get.

4. COPING. A thin projecting cap over the dwarf wall, tinted BRIGHTER than everything below it. Against sky this is the drawn line of the building.

5. KANGURAS (merlons). The decorative crenellation frieze. Wikipedia's Battlement article records the South Asian form exactly: Indian "merlons were typically semicircular and pointed at the top rather than rectangular." They are NOT European square teeth with wide gaps — they are a dense, near-continuous scalloped or spike-like ribbon, in Braj built of lakhori brick and lime plaster and therefore stepped-and-pointed rather than carved. Growse in Mathura uses "battlemented" only for fort-scale work — the sarais at Kosi and Chhata ("fine fort-like buildings, with massive battlemented walls and bastions") and the Mahaban tahsili. He never records kanguras on the old Vrindavan temples. That silence is load-bearing: kanguras belong to the 18th–19th-century Jat/Rajput/Lucknavi layer of Braj, not to the Akbar-period stone series.

6. THE ARCADED PARAPET. The Govind Dev case and the single most specific parapet fact in the entire published survey. Growse: "The loss of the towers and of the lofty arcaded parapet that surmounted the walls has terribly marred the effect of the exterior and given it a heavy stunted appearance." And, crucially, the physical evidence: "the open arcades, which crowned the facade, though not a fragment of them now remains, were probably put up, as the stones of the parapet still show the dents of the pillars." So the crown was a miniature open COLONNADE standing on the coping — small pillars carrying a thin architrave, sky visible through it — and Growse names the Gwalior fort temple as the surviving model. It is GONE and must not be modelled at Govind Dev, but it is the right crown for any building where you want a Man Singh-era stone temple to read as complete.

7. RAILINGS / JALI PARAPETS (kathera). Growse everywhere notes "panels of reticulated tracery (jali)" as Mathura's one indigenous art, and specifically that at his Mathura church "the plain parapet of the aisles would have been replaced by one of carved stone." On terraces people actually stand on — Shahji's terrace, kunj roofs, ghat landings, jharokha fronts — the parapet is a pierced screen between posts, not merlons.

8. CORNER KIOSKS (chhatris). Growse's recurring phrase is "corner kiosks," and he records them on terraces and enclosure walls rather than scattered along roofs: the Bharatpur cenotaphs at Kusum Sarovar are "a lofty and substantial square masonry terrace with corner kiosks and lateral alcoves," their cloister has "a small dome at each corner"; the Pan Sarovar temple stands in a court "surrounded by a lofty wall with corner kiosks"; the Mathura Hardinge arch was given a cupola "with four corner kiosques"; the Kosi town gate is "of stone with two corner turrets." On the Yamuna front he calls them kiosques directly — "all the graceful stone kiosques" of the ghats, which mendicants had blocked up with mud walls and blackened with cooking fires. Sinha & Dhariwal give the ghat rule precisely: "The ghats consist of stairs between pairs of burjes (octagonal piers), extending into the river with tibaris (triple arched pavilion) below and domed chattris above... a rhythm has been established in their repetition along the riverbank."

9. GULDASTAS. The slender octagonal corner pinnacle that terminates a parapet run — a shaft blooming into a small bud with its own tip. Braj practice is documented through its Jat parent at Deeg (Growse explicitly puts the Ganga Mohan Kunj at Keshi Ghat in "precisely the same" style as "the Garden Palace at Dig"), but the published record on guldasta dimensions in Braj specifically is THIN, and the numbers below are derived from the brick module, not measured.

10. FINIALS AND FLAGS. Growse derives the shikhara crown as "the pinnacle with its peculiar base" — amalaka then kalasha. Gilding is documented at exactly two places in his survey and nowhere else: Radha Indra Kishor, where "the sikhara is surmounted with a copper kalas, or finial, heavily gilt, which alone cost Rs. 5,000"; and Rangaji's dhwaja stambha, "a pillar, or dhvaja stambha, of copper gilt, sixty feet in height, and also sunk some twenty-four feet more below the surface of the ground." The saffron flag on a pole, which is the real top of most Vrindavan silhouettes today, is not in Growse at all — it is a photographic fact, and I flag it as such.

11. BANGLADAR. The curved "Bengal" roof, which Growse calls "the curious curvilinear roof, distinctive of the style," recorded over the central compartments of the Kusum Sarovar cloisters, and named by Sinha & Dhariwal as one of the Keshi Ghat constituents. A small vaulted cap, not a dome — useful for breaking a run of flat parapet on kunjs and gate pavilions.

## proportions

Take y = 0 at the ROOF DECK (top of wall, behind the chhajja). All heights are above that line. Where a number is derived rather than surveyed I say so.

--- ANCHOR HEIGHTS FROM GROWSE (the scale everything else is a fraction of) ---
- Madan Mohan nave: 57 ft long (17.4 m), choir and sanctuary 20 ft (6.1 m) square each, "total height would seem to have been only about 22 feet" = 6.7 m to the wall head.
- Harideva, Govardhan (same series, same hand): nave 68 x 20 ft (20.7 x 6.1 m), "the height is about 30 feet to the cornice" = 9.1 m.
- Jugal Kishor: choir 25 ft (7.6 m) square.
- Radha Vallabh: east facade 34 ft (10.4 m) broad, in three stages; hall 63 x 20 ft (19.2 x 6.1 m).
- Rangaji: outer walls 773 x 440 ft (236 x 134 m); west entrance pavilion 93 ft (28.3 m); dhwaja stambha 60 ft (18.3 m) above ground + 24 ft (7.3 m) buried.
- Lala Babu (Krishna Chandrama): 160 ft (48.8 m) frontage.

So a Braj single-storey temple wall head is 6.5–9.1 m, and a two-storey kunj 8–11 m. Scale the parapet to that.

RULE: parapet total = 0.13–0.17 x wall height, clamped to 0.95–1.35 m.
  At Madan Mohan's 6.7 m wall -> 1.05 m (17%). At Harideva's 9.1 m -> 1.18 m (13%).
Without merlons (the plain version) total = 0.66 m; still tint the three courses differently.

--- MERLON (KANGURA) GEOMETRY ---
- base width:        0.42 m
- gap:               0.15 m
- PITCH:             0.57 m   <-- this is the correction that matters most
- height:            0.50 m
- depth:             full parapet thickness 0.24 m at the base, tapering to 0.16 m at the tip
- tip width:         0.14 m (tapered, not square — Indian merlons are "pointed at the top")
- solid fraction:    74%. A kangura row is a near-continuous frieze, NOT isolated teeth.

Your current parapet() at LandmarkGenerator.js:491 uses width 0.45 with pitch 0.90 — 50% solid. That is a European battlement rhythm and it is why the merlons read as a crenellated castle rather than a Braj cornice. Halving the pitch is a one-line change with a large effect.

Brick-module cross-check (lakhori brick, the actual material of every plastered Braj parapet, documented at 150–200 x 90–120 x 25–40 mm, laid in thick lime joints for a ~42 mm course): 0.42 m base = ~3 brick lengths; 0.50 m height = ~12 courses; 0.15 m gap = 1 brick; 0.57 m pitch = 4 bricks. The numbers are buildable in the real material, which is the test they have to pass.

Scale bands:
- temple / haveli / kunj:  base 0.38–0.45, height 0.45–0.55, pitch 0.52–0.62
- gateway or gate-tower:   base 0.50–0.60, height 0.65–0.80, pitch 0.72–0.85
- fort or sarai wall:      base 0.90–1.20, height 1.0–1.4, pitch 1.3–1.7   (Growse's "battlemented walls and bastions" at Kosi/Chhata — this scale is WRONG on a temple)

CORNER RULE: place merlon centres at i x pitch for i = 0..n, so a full merlon lands exactly ON each corner, shared between the two perpendicular runs. Snap n = round(L / 0.57) and recompute pitch = L / n so the run is symmetric. Your current `(i + 0.5) / n - 0.5` guarantees a GAP at every corner, which reads as a chipped tooth from every diagonal view — the view you mostly get.

GATE RULE: raise the whole parapet stack by +0.40 m across the gate bay (2.5–4.0 m wide), with a 0.20 m step at each end of the raise. Per Sinha & Dhariwal, the gokhe is where the emphasis goes; a parapet at one height all the way round is the flattest possible answer.

--- RAILING / JALI PARAPET (terraces people stand on; total 0.92 m) ---
- posts:            0.22 m square, h 0.80, at 1.50 m centres
- jali panel:       h 0.62, thickness 0.10, set back 0.07 from the post face, tint x0.72
- coping:           h 0.12, overhang 0.05, tint x1.06
The dark tint IS the piercing. With flat vertex colour and no textures, a recessed panel at 72% brightness reads as a jali screen from 15 m and costs 10 triangles; actual perforation costs hundreds and is invisible.

--- ARCADED PARAPET (the Govind Dev crown — and see the warning below) ---

- architrave:  h 0.14, over the whole run
- total:       0.99 m

--- GHAT BURJ RHYTHM (Keshi Ghat; Sinha & Dhariwal) ---
- burj (octagonal pier):  4.0 m across flats, rising 4.5–6.0 m from the low-water line
- tibari below:           triple cusped arch, bay 1.6–1.9 m, springing ~2.4 m
- chhatri above:          r 1.6, columns 2.0 (use the numbers above, scaled)
- pairs flank each stair; along-bank pitch 20–25 m
The repetition is the point. One burj is a folly; six in a row is Keshi Ghat.

--- FINIALS AND FLAGS ---
- shikhara kalasha stack: total height 0.10–0.14 x shikhara height; amalaka diameter 0.45–0.50 x shikhara base radius
- Rangaji shala ridge kalashas: pitch 1.3 m, each 0.85 m tall (0.30 base + 0.35 body + 0.20 tip)
- Rangaji dhwaja stambha: 18.3 m above ground, gilt copper (Growse). Temple trust and UP Tourism both say 50 ft / 15.2 m gold-plated. Use 15–18 m and note the conflict.
- saffron flag: pole 3.5–5.0 m above whatever it stands on, pole 0.06 m square, flag 1.6 x 0.7 m. NOT in the published survey — photographic evidence only.

--- TINTING (with flat vertex colour this is half the effect) ---
  kerb x0.88 / dwarf wall x1.00 / coping x1.06 / merlon x1.10 / jali panel x0.72 / chhajja soffit x0.80
The merlons should be the BRIGHTEST element on the building, because they are the only part seen exclusively against sky.

## howToBuildCheaply

Your MeshBuilder at /Users/sushilbajpai/Desktop/Projects/livebraj/client/src/engine/render/MeshBuilder.js already has everything needed. Note cy is the BOTTOM of a box (y0 = 0, y1 = h at line 65), and box() takes a `faces` bitmask — a merlon's underside is never seen, so 0b111101 saves 2 triangles each.

THE KEY INSIGHT: `prism()` (MeshBuilder.js:84) is a tapered box for 12 triangles. A kangura IS a tapered spike. One prism = one correct merlon. You do not need a stack.

  // foot and thinner at the tip. Growse records battlements in Braj only on

  b.box  (mx, y,        mz, 0.42, 0.18, T, tint(color, 1.08), rot, 0b111101);
  b.prism(mx, y + 0.18, mz, 0.42, T, 0.14, T * 0.66, 0.32, tint(color, 1.12), rot);

--- 2. THE PARAPET RUN — replace parapet() at LandmarkGenerator.js:491 ---
  function parapet(b, cx, y, cz, w, d, rot, color, style = 'kangura', raiseAt = null) {
    const T = 0.24, SET = 0.12;           // thickness, and the set-back behind the chhajja
    const ow = w - SET * 2, od = d - SET * 2;
    // three long boxes per course, four runs — twelve boxes for the whole ring
    for (const [ax, az, len, ww, dd, alongX] of runs(ow, od)) {
      const q = local(cx, cz, ax, az, rot);
      b.box(q[0], y,        q[1], ww + 0.10, 0.10, dd + 0.10, tint(color, 0.88), rot, 0b111101);
      b.box(q[0], y + 0.10, q[1], ww,        0.46, dd,        color,              rot, 0b111101);
      b.box(q[0], y + 0.56, q[1], ww + 0.08, 0.10, dd + 0.08, tint(color, 1.06), rot, 0b111101);
      if (style === 'plain') continue;
      // SNAP the pitch so a merlon lands on both ends of the run
      const n = Math.max(2, Math.round(len / 0.57));

        ...prism at (i * pitch - len / 2) along the run, at y + 0.66...
      }
    }

  Cost for a 12 x 9 m temple: 12 course boxes (~144 tris) + ~74 merlons (~888 tris) = ~1,030 tris.
  Sixteen temples: ~16.5k. That is nothing on a mid-range phone, and it is the whole silhouette.

  Budget lever if you need it: merlons only on runs facing a street or the river; plain coping on the back. Pass a bitmask of which of the four runs get kanguras. A back wall nobody circles is 250 triangles you keep.

## mistakes

1. EUROPEAN BATTLEMENT RHYTHM. The commonest and the worst. Your current parapet() uses 0.45 m merlons at a 0.90 m pitch — 50% solid, which is a castle. An Indian kangura row is ~74% solid: 0.42 base, 0.15 gap, 0.57 pitch. Halving the pitch is one number and changes the whole reading.

2. A GAP AT THE CORNER. `(i + 0.5) / n - 0.5` puts a void exactly where two runs meet, and the corner is what you see from every diagonal approach. Put a full merlon ON each corner, shared between the two runs, and snap the pitch so the run is symmetric.

3. FLAT-TOPPED RECTANGULAR MERLONS. The record is explicit that South Asian merlons were "semicircular and pointed at the top rather than rectangular." A prism costs the same 12 triangles as a box and is correct instead of wrong.

4. FORT MERLONS ON A TEMPLE. Growse's "massive battlemented walls and bastions" belong to the Kosi and Chhata sarais at 0.9–1.2 m. Putting that scale on a 7 m temple wall gives you a toy castle. Temple kanguras are 0.40–0.55 m.

5. KANGURAS ON THE AKBAR-PERIOD SERIES. Govind Dev, Madan Mohan, Jugal Kishor, Gopinath, Radha Vallabh. Merlons are an 18th–19th-century Jat/Rajput/Lucknavi import into Braj; Growse never records them on the 1580s stone temples. Applying the vocabulary uniformly to all sixteen buildings would flatten the very chronology the massing research established.

6. REBUILDING GOVIND DEV'S ARCADED PARAPET. It is the best-documented parapet in the survey and it is also the one that no longer exists. Growse: "not a fragment of them now remains." Model the bare wall head — the "heavy stunted appearance" he complains of IS the building as it stands.

7. PARAPET FLUSH WITH THE WALL FACE. Set it back 0.12 m behind the chhajja drip. Flush, it reads as the wall simply getting taller and the eave disappears; set back, you get the stepped profile that says roof.

8. ONE PARAPET HEIGHT ALL THE WAY ROUND. Sinha & Dhariwal are explicit that in this building type "the entry gate known as gokhe is emphasized." Raise the parapet +0.40 m over the gate bay. A constant-height ring is the flattest possible roofline and it will still photograph as a box.

9. MODELLING THE JALI. Do not perforate anything. A recessed panel at 72% brightness reads as pierced tracery from 15 m for 10 triangles; real holes cost hundreds and vanish at phone resolution.

10. CHHATRIS AS SPRINKLES. They go at CORNERS of terraces and enclosure walls, and on the ghat BURJES in repeating pairs. Growse's phrase is always "corner kiosks." Scattering them along a roof ridge is a Bollywood-palace reflex, not Braj.

11. HEMISPHERICAL CHHATRI DOMES. The Mughal-derived dome is stilted: rise ≈ 0.59 x diameter, over a small drum. A true hemisphere reads as a bubble and is the second-commonest dome error after making it too smooth.

12. A CHHATRI WITH NO SHADOW UNDER THE SLAB. Four posts, a slab and a dome is a mushroom. The chhajja ring under the slab, tinted x0.80, is what makes it architecture. Nine triangles.

13. INVENTING GILDING. Growse documents gilt finials at exactly two places: Radha Indra Kishor's copper kalas ("which alone cost Rs. 5,000") and Rangaji's gilt-copper dhwaja stambha. Your JSON already warns "do not invent gilding" at Jugal Kishor and "no gilding, because none is documented" at line 3784. A gold spire on Radha Raman or Madan Mohan is unsourced tourism boilerplate.

14. NO TONAL SEPARATION. With flat vertex colour and no textures, a parapet in the same colour as the wall is invisible. Kerb x0.88, wall x1.00, coping x1.06, merlon x1.10. The merlons must be the brightest thing on the building, because they are the only part with sky behind them.

15. FORGETTING THE FLAG. Saffron flags on poles are the literal top of most Vrindavan silhouettes today. They are not in the 1880s survey — say so in the code comment — but leaving them out makes the town read as a ruin rather than a living one. Sixteen triangles.

16. UNIFORM MERLONS ON ALL FOUR SIDES OF EVERY BUILDING. Real ones face the street and the river; the back of a Braj building is usually a plain coping. This is also your cheapest triangle saving.
