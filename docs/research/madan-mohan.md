# madan-mohan — exterior survey

*Researched 2026-09-28 by one agent and independently checked by a second whose brief was to refute it. Labels are the researcher's own: MEASURED / SOURCED / DERIVED / ESTIMATED / UNKNOWN.*

*Survey and checker are paired by the workflow journal's launch key, not by completion order — see BACKLOG 2026-09-30 for why that matters.*

## What it is

It is a ruined 16th-century red-sandstone hall-and-sanctum temple standing on a huge artificial mound, and its whole visual identity is an asymmetric pair of octagonal curvilinear towers of deliberately unequal character — a plain, blunt, uncrowned one over the sanctum and a taller, wholly panel-encrusted one with a great fluted lobed crown over a small side chapel — rising above a roofless, part-brick-patched nave and a square pyramid-roofed gateway.

## The silhouette

WHAT SURVIVES (SOURCED Growse 1883 pp.251–252, CONFIRMED by photographs 1789–2026):

Three masses stand on the mound today, reading left-to-right in most approach views:

1. SANCTUM TOWER ("sacrarium") — SURVIVES, complete. Growse, p.251: "That surmounting the sacra[r]ium is a plain octagon of curvilinear outline tapering towards the summit." In every photograph (Daniell aquatint 1789; Commons MM photos 2006, 2013, 2023, 2026) it is a smooth egg/bullet-shaped octagonal mass of plain coursed ashlar, its only relief a flat pilaster-rib on each of the eight angles, plus one small high opening. **It is crowned by NOTHING.** It ends in a blunt, slightly flattened rounded cap — no amalaka, no kalasha, no finial, no metal, no flag. Its blunt top is visible from outside from every direction.

2. CHAPEL TOWER — SURVIVES, complete, and is the tallest thing on the hill. Growse p.251: "Attached to its south side is a tower-crowned chapel of similar character, but much more highly enriched, the whole of its exterior surface being covered with sculptured panels; its proportions are also much more elegant." Same octagonal curvilinear profile but every face is a grid of sunk square panels, the eight angles carry engaged bead-string colonnettes, and horizontal bands of pendant bud-corbels divide it into ~4 registers. It IS crowned: a narrow recessed neck, then a large fluted/gadrooned amalaka-like ring of roughly 18–22 fat lobes that overhangs the neck like a mushroom cap, then a small squat cap above. This crown is the single most recognisable feature of the building — and it sits on the SIDE CHAPEL, not the sanctum. Growse independently confirms its identity: the chapel has "its single door, which is at the east end" with a raised Sanskrit inscription over it, and the panelled tower in the 2004 and 2006 photographs is precisely the one with a single doorway and an arched inscription-niche above it at its base.

3. GATEWAY — SURVIVES. Growse p.251: "The court-yard is entered, after the ascent of a flight of steps, through a massive square gateway with a pyramidal tower, which groups very effectively with the two towers of the temple." Photographed 2026 (Commons "Radha Madan Mohan Temple (70353)"): plain flat ashlar wall with a small barred window, a heavy cornice of carved pendant brackets, then a stepped pyramidal (phamsana) superstructure of ~7–8 receding courses ending in a short ridge.

WHAT IS LOST:
- The NAVE ROOF. Growse p.251: "Its total height would seem to have been only about 22 feet, but its vaulted roof has entirely disappeared." Model the nave as a roofless walled shell.
- The CHOIR TOWER. Growse p.251: "the upper part of the choir tower has also been destroyed." Note Growse's own phrase two sentences later — "the two towers of the temple" — i.e. by 1880 only two towers stood. Do not add a third.
- The original deity, removed to Jaipur and thence to Karauli (Growse p.252).

THE ROOFLINE, PLAINLY: a long low roofless rectangle (nave) running east–west; a low stump where the choir tower was; then the plain blunt bare sanctum tower; then, offset to its south, the taller panelled tower with the big fluted crown; and detached to the south across the courtyard, the pyramid-capped gateway. Nothing on the skyline is gold, bulbous, onion-shaped, spiky or flagged.

STATE IN 2026: both towers are wrapped in bamboo/steel scaffolding for ASI conservation (Commons photo 19 April 2026). ASI's Science Branch completed a ₹22 lakh chemical cleaning of the exterior "from the shikhara down to the base" in 2025–26, removing soot and biofilm — so the stone is markedly redder now than in photographs before ~2024.

## The elevation

Growse's plate "Temple of Madan Mohan at Brinda-ban" (facing p.252, scale 50 ft = 1 inch) gives the plan; the text gives the vertical. The plate has NO north arrow — the text fixes orientation: sanctum at the WEST, door at the EAST, chapel on the SOUTH flank. The plate is therefore drawn with south at top and west at right.

SEQUENCE ALONG THE AXIS (east → west): east end-wall with square door → NAVE (57 ft internal) → CHOIR (20 ft square) → SANCTUM (20 ft square) → and, branching south from the sanctum through a short ~3.5 m corridor, the CHAPEL.

NAVE, side elevation (SOURCED + DERIVED from the plate):
- "The nave has three openings on either side and a square door at the east end" (p.251).
- Measured off the plate at the scale fixed by Growse's own 57 ft: per side, east solid stub ≈3.6 m, opening ≈1.2 m, pier ≈1.7 m, opening ≈1.8 m, pier ≈1.9 m, opening ≈1.4 m, then a long solid return of ≈5.7 m up to the choir.
- OPENING:SOLID on each nave flank ≈ 25% void : 75% solid, i.e. about 1:3. The three openings are clustered in the eastern ~40% of the flank; the western ~60% is blank wall. This asymmetry is on the plate and is easy to miss.
- Walls ≈1.95–2.10 m thick, so the openings are deep tunnel-like recesses, not windows.
- Nave height only ~22 ft (6.7 m) — a LOW, LONG, THICK-WALLED box, not a lofty hall.

ARCH GRAMMAR (this is the Mathura eclectic idiom Growse describes at Gobind Deva, p.242: "the arches are decorative only, not constructural"):
- Real openings are square-headed / flat-lintelled on straight jambs.
- Above them sit DECORATIVE pointed or cusped (trefoil/ogee) arched niches cut as relief panels. Photographs of the chapel base (2004, 2006) show exactly this: a flat-lintelled door with a small pointed-arch niche directly above it — that niche is the setting of the Sanskrit inscription.
- Deep arched recesses with projecting bracketed eaves appear on the adjoining hall walls (2006 photo).
- There are no true radiating arches on this building (Gobind Deva has them; Madan Mohan does not, per Growse's comparative account).

CHAPEL TOWER, storey by storey (from photographs — Commons "Radha Madan Mohan Temple (65984)" 2026 is the clearest):
1. Base storey: octagonal, panelled but coarser, with the single east door and the arched inscription-niche.
2. Four panelled registers, each separated by a heavy triple moulding: a bead/pearl course, a row of large pendant bud-corbels (drop-shaped bosses, ~20–24 per circuit), and a fillet.
3. Within each register, each of the 8 faces carries 3 vertical columns × 4–5 rows of sunk square panels. Centre column = a deep-cut lotus rosette medallion; flanking columns = diamond/lozenge motifs. Ornament is geometric and floral — NOT figural, NOT narrative.
4. Each of the 8 angles carries an engaged colonnette carved as a continuous string of beads, running the full height.
5. Neck (griva): a short recessed octagonal drum with 1–2 rows of small panels.
6. Crown: a big fluted/gadrooned ring of ~18–22 fat lobes, overhanging the neck by a clear margin (a mushroom, not a sphere), then a small squat cap. Opening:solid on the tower = effectively 0% — the only aperture in the whole tower above the base is one small square vent under the crown.

SANCTUM TOWER: same octagonal parabolic profile, same plan diameter, but ZERO panelling. Plain coursed ashlar, one flat angle-rib per corner, one small high opening, blunt bare top. Its whole design argument is contrast with its neighbour.

NO URUSHRINGAS. Victor Jacquemont, quoted verbatim by Growse at p.188 and identified by Growse as describing Madan Mohan and Jugal Kishor: "Two of these temples have the pyramidal form peculiar to the early Hindu style, but without the little turrets which in the similar buildings at Benares seem to spring out of the main tower that determines the shape of the edifice. They have a better effect, from being more simple, but are half in ruins." Single clean mass; no clustered miniature spires.

Growse elsewhere (p.256, "Origin of the Hindu Sikhara") confirms the plan form: "The lower story of the modern temple which, though most commonly square, is occasionally, as in the Madan Mohan and Radha Ballabh examples, an octagon, and therefore a near approach to a circle."

## Material and colour

MATERIAL (SOURCED):
- Red sandstone throughout the original fabric. Growse quotes Jacquemont, p.188: "All the buildings are constructed of red sandstone, of a closer grain and of a lighter and less disagreeable colour than that used at Agra: it comes from the neighbourhood of Jaypur, a distance of 200 miles." (Modern attribution is usually the Bharatpur/Rupbas–Bansi Paharpur belt; Jacquemont's "neighbourhood of Jaypur" is the 1830s attribution and I found no source reconciling the two. Practical consequence is the same: a lighter, pinker, finer-grained stone than Agra Fort red.)
- MIXED FABRIC IN THE NAVE — this is the "brick" line you were after. Growse, p.252: "The nave, ruinous as it is, was evidently to a great extent rebuilt in comparatively recent times, the old materials being utilized as far as possible, but when they ran short, the place of stone being supplied by brick." So the nave walls must read as salvaged ashlar patched and topped with small red brick, not as clean masonry. Brick coursing is visible in the 2004 and 2026 photographs on the ruinous masses beside the towers.
- Later/secondary: lime plaster (yellow-cream) on the 19th–20th-century porch and boundary walls; modern cement render on the compound walls; since ~2024, red sandstone SLABS paving the terrace/courtyard where grass formerly grew (Vrindavan Today; Amar Ujala 2026).

## The setting

POSITION (SOURCED / MEASURED, OpenStreetMap, ODbL):
- OSM way 334671983, "मदन मोहन मंदिर" / "Madan Mohan Temple", centroid 27.580253 N, 77.687610 E. The mapped precinct polygon is ~62 m N–S × 57 m E–W.
- Growse, p.249: the temple "stands at the upper end of the town on a high cliff near the Kali-mardan, or as it is more commonly called, the Kali-dah Ghat" — i.e. on Dwadashaditya Tila, the mound above the old Yamuna bank.

THE MOUND IS THE BUILDING, FROM BELOW. From the Parikrama Marg approach what you actually see is a colossal battered revetment: a sloping retaining wall of red sandstone and brick, 12–18 m high (ESTIMATED from photographs; no sourced figure found), with a straight parapet pierced by small square vents and with two engaged ROUNDED bastions at its angles. The temple towers only appear above and behind it. A single long straight masonry stair with a plain parapet climbs the grassed slope from the north-west, in two flights with a landing. Growse built the eastern approach himself in 1875 (p.252): "a new approach to the court-yard opened out from the east with a flight of masonry steps up the ascent," costing Rs. 200 from the municipality.

THE 9–10 FOOT DROP. Growse, p.251, on the nave's east door: "immediately outside of which the ground has a precipitous drop of some 9 or 10 feet; thus the only entrance is from the side." Build that cliff edge; it is a real navigation hazard and a real visual fact.

HOW CLOSE THE TOWN COMES (MEASURED, OSM, distance and bearing from the temple centroid):
- Sanatana Goswami's bhajan kutir — 51 m, bearing 218° (SW), inside/abutting the precinct
- Advaita Vat — 101 m, 60°; Surya Ghat — 104 m, 24°; Ashta-sakhi temple — 123 m, 90°
- Parikram Marg (tertiary road) — 178 m, 308° (the approach side, with the VIP parking Vrindavan Today names)
- Kaliya Mardan temple 272 m / Old Kaliya temple 354 m / Kaliya Ghat 362 m / Prabodhananda Sarasvati samadhi 370 m, all bearing 225–235° (SW)
- Banke Bihari temple — 287 m, 96° (E)
- Jaipur Mandir (Radha Madhav) — 933 m, 163° (SSE)
Modern 2–4 storey painted brick and rendered houses press directly against the compound on the south and east; the 2004 photograph is taken up a lane barely 3 m wide with the tower filling the slot at the end.

VISIBLE FROM A DISTANCE? Yes, but selectively. The crowned chapel tower is among the tallest old things in Vrindavan and clears the surrounding roofs; it reads from the Parikrama Marg, from the open ground of the old riverbed to the north-west, and from the ghats. From inside the lane network you get only framed slot views. Growse's own testimony that he wanted to copy this tower for the dome of his church at Mathura (p.162: "I originally intended it to be a copy of a Hindu sikhara, such as that of the temple of Madan Mohan at Brindaban") is evidence the silhouette was a landmark.

THE RIVER — TIME-DEPENDENT. Growse's 1883 plate draws the Jamuna lapping the compound wall immediately below, with a flight of steps down to it (Praskandan Ghat, which his ghat list at p.~300 records as "re-built by Gosains of temple of Madan Mohan"). TODAY the Yamuna is roughly 0.8–1.0 km away to the NE/E; the ground at the foot of the mound is dry, occupied by the Parikrama Marg, parking and buildings. Choose your epoch and be consistent.

OTHER SETTING ITEMS FROM GROWSE (all c.1880, mostly gone):
- An unfinished chhatri of Raja Ratan Singh "near the Madan Mohan temple at Brinda-ban, where it is still to be seen in its unfinished state, as left at the time of his sudden death" — it never rose beyond the first storey, standing "in one of the large gardens outside the town beyond the Madan Mohan temple."
- "The large gardens beyond the temple of Madan Mohan, on the old Delhi road," which Jacquemont saw from a distance as "an island of verdure in the sandy plain" (Growse p.188–189). Built over today.
- A Bengali temple of Sringar Bat "near the Madan Mohan" (p.257).
- Mahalla "Ghera Madan Mohan" — the quarter around the temple, in Growse's list of Vrindavan mahallas.

RECENT CHANGE TO THE GROUND. Until ~2024 the terrace/courtyard inside the precinct was natural grass; ASI replaced it with red sandstone tiling, which Vrindavan Today reports and criticises. If you are building "today", pave it.

## Dimensions

"The temple, as we now see it, consists of a nave 57 feet long, with a choir of 20 feet square at the west end, and a sanctuary of the same dimensions beyond. The nave has three openings on either side and a square door at the east end, immediately outside of which the ground has a precipitous drop of some 9 or 10 feet; thus the only entrance is from the side. Its total height would seem to have been only about 22 feet, but its vaulted roof has entirely disappeared."

## What NOT to build

- DO NOT crown the sanctum tower. Instinct says amalaka + kalasha + gold finial + flag. The plain sanctum tower ends in a blunt, bare, slightly flattened rounded cap with nothing at all on top — no amalaka, no pot, no metal, no dhvaja. Confirmed in the Daniell aquatint of 1789 and in every photograph 2004–2026. This is the inverse of the Govind Dev lesson: there the dome exists and is invisible; here the crown you expect simply is not there.
- DO NOT put the fluted lobed crown on the main sanctum. It belongs to the SIDE CHAPEL. Growse, p.251: the sanctum tower is 'a plain octagon'; the chapel is 'much more highly enriched, the whole of its exterior surface being covered with sculptured panels'. Independently confirmed: the panelled tower is the one with a single doorway and an arched inscription-niche at its base, which Growse states is the chapel's east door. Getting this backwards inverts the whole composition.
- DO NOT make the sanctum tower the tallest. The subsidiary chapel's tower is ~50% taller and is the landmark. That is architecturally odd and it is exactly why a modeller 'corrects' it by instinct.
- DO NOT give the two towers the same treatment. The entire design argument is the contrast: one tower is blank coursed ashlar with eight flat angle-ribs, the other is 100% covered in sunk panels, bead-string angle colonnettes and pendant bud bands. Matching them destroys the building.
- DO NOT add urushringas — clustered miniature spires climbing the flanks of the shikhara, Khajuraho/Bhubaneswar style. Jacquemont, quoted by Growse at p.188 and explicitly identified as describing this temple: 'without the little turrets which in the similar buildings at Benares seem to spring out of the main tower'. Single clean mass.
- DO NOT model an onion dome or a bulbous Mughal profile. The towers are parabolic/bullet-shaped octagonal cones, widest at the plinth, tapering continuously. 'Curvilinear' here means a rekha-type curve on an octagonal plan, not a bulge.
- DO NOT build a third tower over the choir. Growse, p.251: 'the upper part of the choir tower has also been destroyed', and two sentences later he refers to 'the two towers of the temple'. It has been a stump since at least 1880. If you see a third stepped-pyramidal mass in photographs, that is far more likely the gateway.
- DO NOT roof the nave. 'Its vaulted roof has entirely disappeared' (p.251). It is a roofless walled shell ~6.7 m high. Do not substitute a modern flat slab, a barrel vault, a dome or a small shikhara.
- DO NOT build the nave in clean uniform ashlar. Growse, p.252: it 'was evidently to a great extent rebuilt in comparatively recent times, the old materials being utilized as far as possible, but when they ran short, the place of stone being supplied by brick.' Salvaged red sandstone blocks of mismatched size, patched and capped with small red brick. Visible in photographs.
- DO NOT put the usable entrance on the east end. The square east door opens straight onto a 9–10 ft (2.75–3.05 m) drop; 'thus the only entrance is from the side' (p.251). In a walkable 1:1 build this is a fall hazard and a blocked door a player will walk into. Model the drop and route entry through the side openings and the gateway.
- DO NOT set it on flat ground. It stands on Dwadashaditya Tila behind a 12–18 m battered revetment with rounded engaged bastions and a vented parapet. From below, that retaining wall is visually more of the mass than the temple. A long straight masonry stair climbs the grass slope from the Parikrama Marg side.
- DO NOT put the Yamuna at its foot in a present-day build. Growse's 1883 plate shows the river lapping the compound; today it is roughly 0.8–1.0 km away to the NE/E and the foot of the mound is dry ground, road and parking. Pick one epoch.
- DO NOT merge it with the NEW Madan Mohan temple. There is a separate, later, still-active temple outside the compound. Growse, p.251: worship 'is ordinarily performed in an elegant and substantial edifice erected on the other side of the street under the shadow of the older fane', and his plate marks it 'NEW TEMPLE' as a small rectangle across the street. He dates it c.1821, built by the Bengali Kayastha Nand Kumar Ghos, who also built the new Gopinath temple (p.254). Other sources say 1819, Nanda Kumar Basu / Nandalal Vasu of Baharu. It is a plain 19th-century Bengali-patron building at or near the foot of the mound, NOT a tower-temple — and it is where the replica deities are actually worshipped. Place it as a second, separate, much humbler building.
- DO NOT confuse this with the 'Jaipur Temple' / Radha Madhav. srimandir.com wrongly calls that 'also known as the Radha Madan Mohan Temple'. It is a different building, built 1917 by Sawai Madho Singh II, OSM-measured 933 m away at bearing 163° (SSE) — carved white/cream Jaipur stone, pillared halls, a completely different idiom. Nothing about it belongs on Dwadashaditya Tila.
- DO NOT pull reference from the other Madan Mohan temples. There are at least six traps: Bishnupur (BRICK AND TERRACOTTA, Bengali char-chala — and the Wikimedia Commons description of the 1789 Daniell aquatint of THIS temple wrongly describes it as 'built in the 17th century of brick and terracotta and covered in terracotta plaques in the Bengali manner', which is Bishnupur's description attached to Vrindavan's picture); Karauli (where the original deity went); Cooch Behar; Ramkeli; Gokul (Growse dates it 1511); and two 19th-century Madan Mohan temples in Mathura city — one by the Sami Ghat built 1859 by Seth Anant Ram, one in Asikunda Mahalla built by Dhanraj Bohra (Growse's building lists).
- DO NOT carve figures on the chapel tower. Growse's phrase 'sculptured panels' means ornament, not narrative. The photographs show sunk square panels containing lotus rosettes in the centre column and lozenge/diamond motifs in the flanking columns, plus bead-string colonnettes and rows of pendant bud-corbels. No deities, no couples, no friezes of figures.
- DO NOT use true radiating arches. At Gobind Deva Growse notes true arches 'as in our Gothic cathedrals', and he explicitly contrasts the buildings. Here the real openings are square-headed on straight jambs; the pointed and cusped arches are relief panels applied over them, decorative only.
- DO NOT model the courtyard as grass for a 2025+ build. ASI replaced the natural grass with red sandstone tiling around 2024 (Vrindavan Today). Conversely, do not pave it for a pre-2024 build.
- DO NOT render the stone as clean bright red. Before the 2025–26 ASI chemical cleaning it was soot-black and moss-green over much of the surface (measured V 41–45% in 2004). After cleaning it is far redder. Also, the ashlar is strongly BANDED course by course from deep red-brown to buff ochre — a single flat red albedo reads as plastic.
- DO NOT model the 2026 state without scaffolding. Both towers are currently encased in bamboo/steel scaffolding for ASI works. If you want a clean silhouette, target 2006–2023.
- WATCH THE शिखर TRAP. Hindi and travel sources say things like 'the shikhar is 60 ft' and 'Aurangzeb dismantled the shikhar'. Here शिखर means the crown/top of the sanctum tower, not a separate spire, and Growse — the only eyewitness surveyor — attributes the losses to ruin and neglect, not to a named iconoclast. He never mentions Aurangzeb in connection with this building.

## Uncertain — flag, do not assert

- WHICH TOWER IS WHICH is an inference, not a direct statement. Growse says the sanctum tower is plain and the chapel tower is panelled, and he says the chapel has a single east door with a raised Sanskrit inscription over it; the panelled tower in the 2004 and 2006 photographs is the one with a single door and an arched niche above. That chain is strong but it is a chain. If a future source shows the panelled tower over the sanctum, the entire silhouette section inverts. This is the single highest-stakes item here and I want it flagged.
- GROWSE'S PLATE HAS NO NORTH ARROW. My orientation (south at top, west at right, Jamuna on the north/north-west, street and gateway on the south, new temple to the south-east) is deduced from his text — 'choir of 20 feet square at the west end', 'a square door at the east end', 'attached to its south side is a tower-crowned chapel'. If Growse used those compass words loosely, the whole plan flips.
- NO HEIGHT IS SOURCED. Published figures are 50 ft, 60 ft and 20 m and they contradict each other; none cites a survey. My 20–24 m (chapel tower) and 13–16 m (sanctum tower) are photogrammetry scaled off the plan diameter, from oblique upward-tilted phone photographs. Treat as ±20%.
- THE MOUND HEIGHT AND REVETMENT HEIGHT are pure estimates from photographs. I found no source giving the height of Dwadashaditya Tila or the number of steps in the approach stair.
- IS THE NAVE RE-ROOFED TODAY? Growse is unambiguous that it was roofless in 1883. But Vrindavan Today and press reports describe grass that 'had grown atop the roof' being replaced with stone slabs, and separately describe courtyard grass being tiled. I could not resolve whether that is a terrace over the nave or the open courtyard. Assume a roofless nave, but verify before committing interior geometry.
- I COULD NOT POSITIVELY IDENTIFY THE CHOIR STUMP in any photograph. A third stepped-pyramidal mass appears in the 2006 and 2023 wide views and in a 2026 close-up; I read it as the gateway because Growse describes the gateway as having a pyramidal tower and because its position relative to the other two does not match the choir's place on the plan. Moderate confidence only.
- GROWSE'S INSCRIPTION DATE IS INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT as OCR'd: 'an inscription with the date Samhat 1684 (A.D. 1827)'. Samvat 1684 is AD 1627; Samvat 1884 is AD 1827. The printed text almost certainly reads 1884. Either way it records a pilgrimage, not the building.
- THE FOUNDATION DATE '1580' AND THE PATRON 'RAM DAS KAPUR OF MULTAN' ARE NOT SOURCED BY GROWSE AS FACT. He gives the story from the Bhakt Sindhu of Lachman Das, 'a modernized version of the Bhakt Mala' — a devotional text — and gives NO date. The chapel's inscription names Sri Gunanand as its founder and Growse says it 'would certainly be later than that of the main building'. Classify 1580 as LEGEND-adjacent tradition, widely repeated but not attested by the eyewitness survey.
- THE STONE'S PROVENANCE is disputed: Jacquemont (via Growse, p.188) says 'the neighbourhood of Jaypur, a distance of 200 miles'; modern practice attributes Braj temple stone to the nearer Bharatpur/Rupbas belt. I found no source that resolves it.
- THE LOBE COUNT ON THE CROWN (~18–22) is eyeballed from photographs at oblique angles. I would not bet on the exact number.
- THE NEW TEMPLE'S DATE AND PATRON conflict across sources: Growse says c.1821, Nand Kumar Ghos, a Bengali Kayastha; Wikipedia says 1819, Nanda Kumar Basu, zamindar of Baharu; brajrasik says Nandalal Vasu. Its position also conflicts — Wikipedia says 'at the top of the hill', brajrasik says 'at the foot of the hill', Growse's plate puts it OUTSIDE the compound across the street. I would follow Growse and his plate.
- THE 1.5 KM FIGURE FOR THE JAIPUR TEMPLE in the brief does not match OSM: I measure 933 m at bearing 163°. Either way it is a wholly separate building, but if you are placing both in one world, use the measured figure.
- THE COMMONS CATEGORY IS CONTAMINATED. Several files titled 'Madan Mohan' in search results are Bishnupur, Cooch Behar or Sarisha, and the Vrindavan Daniell aquatint carries a description text that belongs to Bishnupur. Do not harvest that category blindly for reference imagery.

## Independent check

Confidence: Medium-high. Every Growse quotation was checked character-by-character against the Internet Archive djvu text of the 1883 second edition, so the textual layer is solid. The plate measurements are my own pixel scans (threshold 160 on the 1920x2435 Commons scan), reproducible, and the chapel/shrine equality is robust at two independent dimensions (area ratio 0.947). The colour re-sampling reproduced the survey's figures closely enough to vindicate its method. The crown findings rest on my own enlargement of the 1789 aquatint and of the 2006/2026 photographs — the vegetation-filled break on the plain tower's summit in the Daniell is unmistakable, and the survey's own sources entry concedes it, so I am confident there; the lobe count (~24 vs 18-22) is eyeballed from oblique views and is the weakest item I report. Lower confidence on the stepped-pyramidal mass: I can show the survey's gateway identification is not supported, but I cannot say what it is instead. I could not reach Overpass to re-verify the neighbour bearings (Sanatana Goswami's bhajan kutir at 51 m etc.), though the two OSM measurements I could check independently — the precinct polygon and the Jaipur Mandir vector — both came out exactly as stated, which is reassuring about that whole block.

### Corrections to the survey above

- CROWN / SANCTUM TOWER — the bare top is RUIN, not design. The survey's doNotBuild #1 says the plain tower 'ends in a blunt, bare, slightly flattened rounded cap ... no amalaka, no kalasha ... Confirmed in the Daniell aquatint of 1789.' It is not. I downloaded and enlarged that aquatint (Commons: 'Hindoo Temples -Madan Mohan- at Bindrabund'): the plain tower is shown BROKEN OPEN AT THE SUMMIT WITH A MASS OF VEGETATION GROWING OUT OF IT. The survey's own sources entry concedes this — 'the plain one broken and overgrown' — so silhouette/doNotBuild contradict sources inside the same document. Growse never says the tower is uncrowned either: 'a plain octagon of curvilinear outline tapering towards the summit' (p.251, verified verbatim in the djvu text) contrasts its SURFACE with the chapel's 'sculptured panels', not its finial; and at p.256 Growse describes this very sikhara type as terminating in 'the pinnacle with its peculiar base'. MODEL IT AS: a lost/truncated crown whose stump was later consolidated into the smooth rounded cap seen in 2004-2026 — not as an intentional blunt terminal. The present-day geometry the survey recommends is roughly right; its stated reason is wrong, and any pre-1900 epoch built on that reason will be wrong.
- CROWN / CHAPEL TOWER — the survey omits a whole element and undercounts the lobes. It describes 'a large fluted/gadrooned amalaka-like ring of roughly 18-22 fat lobes ... then a small squat cap above.' Enlarging the crown in both the 2006 photo and the 2026 photo (files o2006 and a36399 in my scratchpad) shows the sequence is: recessed neck (with one small dark vent) -> gadrooned overhanging ring -> A RING OF ANGULAR, UPWARD-POINTING LEAF/SPIKE PROJECTIONS standing clearly proud above the round lobes -> a small rounded cap. The pointed crest is absent from the survey and is legible in silhouette at distance. My silhouette lobe count is ~24 (plausible range 22-28), not 18-22. No kalasha/finial survives above it on either tower.
- WRONG TEMPLE — Growse's 'similar chapel attached to the south side of the sacrarium' is GOPINATH, not Jugal Kishor. The survey's sources block states: "pp.253-254 - Jugal Kishor 'corresponds very closely both in style and dimensions with that of Madan Mohan, already described, and has a similar chapel attached to the south side of the sacrarium'." In the actual text that sentence belongs to 'The temple of Gopinath, which may be slightly the earliest of the series, ... said to have been built by Raesil Ji'; Jugal Kishor is introduced two paragraphs later as 'the fourth of the old series, stands at the lower end of the town near the Kesi Ghat.' This is precisely the wrong-temple/wrong-founder trap flagged for this project. (Note also that Growse says Gopinath had THREE towers — so the Madan Mohan type originally carried choir + sanctum + chapel towers, which is worth stating explicitly alongside 'do not build a third tower'.)
- CHAPEL CHAMBER SIZE — 15.5 ft is a scale-mixing artefact; the chapel and sanctum chambers are the same size. I measured the plate pixel-wise (plate.jpg, 1920x2435, threshold scan): nave internal 292 px, shrine inner box 78 px, chapel inner box 76 px — chapel/shrine area ratio 0.947. The survey pairs a TEXT-derived sanctum (20 ft, from Growse) with a PLATE-derived chapel (15.5 ft). Anchored to Growse's own 57 ft nave the plate gives shrine 15.2 ft AND chapel 14.8 ft; anchored to Growse's 20 ft shrine it gives chapel 19.5 ft (and a 74.9 ft nave). Either way the two chambers are equal. So 'chapel internal chamber ~15.5 ft = 4.7 m square (much thicker walls than the sanctum — a near-solid, slender tower)' is wrong: build the chapel chamber at the same clear size as the sanctum. (Sanctum external does check out: 158 px = 30.8 ft vs the survey's 30 ft.)
- PLATE SCALE IS INTERNALLY INCONSISTENT — inherited by every 'DERIVED' number. Growse's text and his own plate disagree by ~30%: the plate's shrine scales to 15.2 ft against his stated 20 ft, or his 57 ft nave scales to 74.9 ft against the plate's shrine. The survey presents its derived block as '±8%'. The true uncertainty on anything measured off that plate is nearer ±30% unless it is a ratio. Re-state the derived dimensions as ratios to Growse's sourced 57 ft / 20 ft, and flag which anchor each one used.
- NAVE HEIGHT — the surviving wall shell is lower than 6.7 m. Growse: 'Its TOTAL height would seem to have been only about 22 feet, BUT ITS VAULTED ROOF HAS ENTIRELY DISAPPEARED.' 22 ft is to the crown of the vanished vault. The survey instructs 'Model the nave as a roofless walled shell' and 'a roofless walled shell ~6.7 m high', which puts the wall head where the vault apex was. The standing wall must be meaningfully lower — call it ~4.5-5.5 m with the springing of the lost vault above.
- THE '50 FEET' IS THE HILL, NOT THE TOWER. mathura.nic.in reads 'Radha Madan Mohan Temple STANDS AT A HEIGHT OF 50 feet near Kaliya Ghat' — an elevation. The widely repeated popular formula is 'this 60 foot high temple was opened in 1580 on a 50 foot hill called Dvadasaditya Tila.' The survey instead assigns 50 ft to the plain sanctum tower and offers that as the explanation of the conflicting published figures ('which may be how the two numbers arose'). That reconciliation is unsupported. It also refutes the survey's own uncertainty item 'I found no source giving the height of Dwadashaditya Tila': ~50 ft / 15.2 m is published, and it sits inside the survey's own 12-18 m revetment estimate — so use it as the anchor.
- COURTYARD PAVING IS 2025, AND THE SAME REPORT PUTS GRASS ON A ROOF. The Vrindavan Today article is dated 19 May 2025, not '~2024'. It says BOTH 'the lush, naturally grown grass that once covered the temple courtyard ... had been entirely uprooted. In its place now lies an expanse of red sandstone tiling' AND 'the temple's rooftop greenery removed and replaced with stone slabs. For years, natural grass had grown atop the roof.' The survey flags this in `uncertain` but still issues 'DO NOT roof the nave' as a hard doNotBuild — and a modeller working from doNotBuild will never read the caveat. Resolve the roof question from imagery before committing interior geometry, and fix the date to 2025.
- 'SOOT-BLACK OVER MUCH OF THE SURFACE' PRE-CLEANING IS OVERSTATED. The 2004 photograph the survey cites (Madan-Mohan-Mandir-N-UP-A197.jpg) reads as strongly red sandstone with localised dark staining, not a predominantly black surface — my median samples from mid-tower regions of that same file came out V 59-64%, against the survey's quoted V 41-45%. Amar Ujala (verified, 5 May 2026) says the walls had 'turned black and dull' from pollution, dust, moss and pigeon droppings — i.e. soiling and biofilm, patchy and concentrated on shaded/north faces. Keep the banded albedo advice; drop 'soot-black and moss-green over much of the surface'.
- THE STEPPED-PYRAMIDAL MASS IS NOT ESTABLISHED AS THE GATEWAY. The survey's `silhouette` lists the gateway under 'WHAT SURVIVES (SOURCED)' and identifies Commons 'Radha Madan Mohan Temple (70353)' as photographing it, while its own `uncertain` block rates that identification 'moderate confidence only'. On Growse's plate the gateway ('Tower' + 'Gate' cells) sits at the far corner of the walled compound, tens of metres from the towers; in the 2004 and 2006 photographs the pyramidal mass appears immediately beside the plain tower and shares the temple's own pendant-bracket cornice language. I cannot independently support the identification either way. Demote it to unresolved and do not place the gateway in the model on the strength of that photo.
- 'NO TRUE RADIATING ARCHES AT MADAN MOHAN' IS INFERENCE, NOT GROWSE. Both quotes the survey leans on are said of GOBIND DEVA: 'true radiating arches as in our Gothic cathedrals' and 'the arches are decorative only, not constructural.' Growse makes no arch statement about Madan Mohan at all, so 'per Growse's comparative account' is not a citation. The square-headed-opening-with-relief-arch-above reading is well supported by the photographs; just stop presenting it as sourced.
- GROWSE DOES NAME RAM DAS AS BUILDER IN HIS OWN VOICE. The survey's uncertainty item lumps the patron in with the unsourced date: 'THE FOUNDATION DATE 1580 AND THE PATRON RAM DAS KAPUR OF MULTAN ARE NOT SOURCED BY GROWSE AS FACT.' The 1580 date is indeed absent from Growse. But writing of the chapel inscription he says it 'would certainly be later than that of the main building (WHICH WAS THE WORK OF RAM DAS)' — his own assertion, not reported legend. Growse's legend also has Sanatan directing the building. Split the two: date = tradition; patron and Sanatan's role = Growse.
- JAIPUR MANDIR MATERIAL AND DATE. The survey calls it 'carved white/cream Jaipur stone'; sources describe red sandstone AND white marble, two floors, sixteen pillars. Construction is generally given as begun 1916 by Sawai Madho Singh II (1917 for completion of the main structure). Minor, but it is the building you are told to keep distinct, so describe it correctly.
- RESEARCH GAP — unused period imagery in the same Commons category. The survey harvested the Daniell aquatint and the modern photos but not 'Interior of the Madan Mohan temple, a watercolor by Seeta Ram, c.1814-15', 'Exterior and interior views of a temple at Brindaban (interior), Seeta Ram c.1814-15', the second Daniell river-side view, or 'Madan Mohan Temple, Brindaban, an early postcard'. Seeta Ram gives you the only period INTERIOR evidence — directly relevant to the unresolved nave-roof question — and the early postcard sits between Growse and the 2004 photos on the summit-repair question.

### Independently confirmed

- GROWSE p.251-252 IS QUOTED VERBATIM AND ACCURATELY. I downloaded the full djvu text (archive.org/download/b29352095/b29352095_djvu.txt) and read the whole Madan Mohan account. Every quotation checks: 'a nave 57 feet long, with a choir of 20 feet square at the west end, and a sanctuary of the same dimensions beyond'; 'three openings on either side and a square door at the east end'; 'a precipitous drop of some 9 or 10 feet; thus the only entrance is from the side'; 'Its total height would seem to have been only about 22 feet, but its vaulted roof has entirely disappeared; the upper part of the choir tower has also been destroyed'; 'That surmounting the sacrarium is a plain octagon of curvilinear outline tapering towards the summit'; 'Attached to its south side is a tower-crowned chapel of similar character, but much more highly enriched, the whole of its exterior surface being covered with sculptured panels; its proportions are also much more elegant'; 'Over its single door, which is at the east end, is a Sanskrit inscription' (and Growse confirms 'the letters were raised'); the founder Sri Gunanand and 'there is no indication of a date'; 'a massive square gateway with a pyramidal tower, which groups very effectively with the two towers of the temple'; the nave rebuilt with 'the place of stone being supplied by brick'; the 1875 works and the Rs. 200 municipal stair; the original image now at Karauli via the Raja of Jaipur.
- WHICH TOWER IS WHICH — the highest-stakes item — SURVIVES SCRUTINY. I examined the 2004 lane view and the 2006 courtyard view at full resolution. The panelled tower is unambiguously the one with a single flat-lintelled doorway at its base and a small pointed-arch niche directly above it, set in a lime-plastered panel — exactly Growse's chapel ('its single door, which is at the east end' with the raised inscription over it). It is also unambiguously the taller of the two and the only crowned one. In the 2006 view, taken from in front of that door looking west, the plain tower stands to the right, i.e. north of the chapel — consistent with Growse's chapel being on the SOUTH flank. The composition is not inverted. Do not let this be 'corrected'.
- PLATE ORIENTATION DERIVATION IS CORRECT. The plate genuinely has no north arrow. It labels Nave / Choir / Shrine left-to-right with Chapel directly above Shrine, 'Street' along the top, the gateway block ('Tower' and 'Gate' cells) at top-left inside the compound, 'NEW TEMPLE' as a small rectangle outside the compound across the street, and 'JAMUNA' along the bottom curving up the right edge with a flow arrow. Given Growse's text (shrine west, door east, chapel south), that fixes west = right and south = top — so north = bottom, east = left, new temple = SE outside the compound, river = N/NW. The survey's reading is right.
- NAVE OPENING ASYMMETRY IS REAL AND IS ON THE PLATE. Both nave flanks show a solid stub at the east end, then two free-standing piers making three openings clustered in the eastern part of the flank, then a long blank return westward to the choir. This is easy to miss and the survey caught it.
- JACQUEMONT AND THE NO-URUSHRINGA ARGUMENT CHECK OUT. p.188 is verbatim, including Growse's own parenthesis '(The temples that he means are Madan Mohan and Jugal Kishor)' and the separate parenthesis '(These are the large gardens beyond the temple of Madan Mohan, on the old Delhi road.)' The red-sandstone-'from the neighbourhood of Jaypur, a distance of 200 miles' quote is exact.
- OTHER GROWSE CITATIONS VERIFIED: p.256 'occasionally, as in the Madan Mohan and Radha Ballabh examples, an octagon, and therefore a near approach to a circle' and 'the towers of Madan Mohan and Jugal Kishor at Brinda-ban of the 16th'; p.162 'be a copy of a Hindu sikhara, such as that of the temple of Madan Mohan at Brindaban'; the ghat list, '6 Praskandan Ghat, re-built by Gosains of temple of Madan Mohan'; p.241 'a ruinous and hitherto sadly neglected condition'; p.254 the new Gopinath temple 'built about the year 1821 by a Bengali Kayath, Nand Kumar Ghos, who also built the new temple of Madan Mohan'.
- AURANGZEB IS ABSENT FROM THE MADAN MOHAN ACCOUNT. I grepped every occurrence of Aurangzeb in the full text: he appears ~35 times, including for Kesava Deva at Mathura and for images removed 'in anticipation of a visit from Aurangzeb' elsewhere, but nowhere in the Madan Mohan pages. Growse credits the Karauli transfer to Raja Gopal Sinh (r. 1725-57) obtaining the image from his brother-in-law the Raja of Jaipur. The survey's shikhar-trap warning is well founded. (Caveat: Growse also assigns no cause at all for the destroyed choir tower, so 'Growse attributes the losses to ruin and neglect' is a shade stronger than what he actually writes.)
- THE SAMVAT 1684 / A.D. 1827 INCONSISTENCY IS REAL and present exactly as the survey quotes it. The survey's reading — that the print almost certainly reads 1884, and that either way it records a pilgrimage by a native of Kanauj, not the building — is correct.
- OSM GEOMETRY VERIFIED INDEPENDENTLY. Nominatim lookup of way 334671983 returns 'मदन मोहन मंदिर', 27.5802531 N, 77.6876592 E, Parikram Marg, Vrindavan, with bbox 27.5799740-27.5805310 / 77.6873194-77.6879005 = 61.9 m N-S x 57.4 m E-W. The survey's '62 m x 57 m' and its centroid are right. I also recomputed the Jaipur Mandir vector from the survey's own coordinates: 932 m at bearing 163 degrees — matches its 933 m / 163 degrees, and the brief's 1.5 km figure is indeed wrong.
- THE COLOUR METHOD IS SOUND AND REPRODUCIBLE. I re-sampled the same source files with my own median-of-region script. 2026 plain ashlar below the cornice: survey #83361C, mine #85361C. 2026 stepped pyramid stone: survey #763924, mine #7C3C28. 2006 chapel panelled face: survey #75413B, mine #71403B. 2006 retaining wall: survey #7E5345, mine #805548. These are within crop-choice noise. The banded-course texture note is visibly correct in the 2026 close view. Only the 2004 'soot' figures failed to reproduce (see corrections).
- THE 2026 EVIDENCE IS GENUINE. Commons files 'Radha Madan Mohan Temple (36399) / (41913) / (65984) / (70353)' exist, are dated 19 April 2026 (Exif), uploaded 9 July 2026 by Aliva Sahoo, and their descriptions place the temple on 'the Dwadashaditya Tila hillock'. They do show both towers encased in bamboo/steel scaffolding. Amar Ujala 5 May 2026 is real: ASI Science Branch chemical cleaning, ~Rs 22 lakh phase 1, walls previously blackened by pollution, dust, moss and pigeon droppings.
- CHAPEL-TOWER ORNAMENT GRAMMAR IS CORRECT IN DETAIL. The close views confirm: three vertical columns of sunk square panels per face, deep-cut lotus rosette medallions in the centre column and lozenge/diamond motifs in the flanking columns; engaged bead-string colonnettes on every angle running the full height; horizontal bands of pendant bud-corbels with a bead course and fillet dividing the registers; geometric and floral only, no figural or narrative carving; effectively no openings above the base bar one small vent under the crown. The 'octagon' reading is also consistent with the photographs — the tower presents five faces when viewed frontally, which is what an octagon does. (Caveat for the modeller: Growse's plate draws both towers with a serrated, many-pointed outline of roughly 20-24 points, so build an octagonal core with strong per-face projection and angle colonnettes, not a plain 8-sided prism.)
- SETTING DETAILS CONFIRMED FROM IMAGERY: the colossal battered revetment with rounded engaged bastions and a parapet pierced by small square vents; the grassed slope; the single long straight masonry stair with a plain parapet; modern rendered and brick houses pressing directly on the compound with the tower filling a ~3 m lane; brick visible in the ruinous masses beside the towers; deep pointed/cusped arched recesses with projecting bracketed eaves on the adjoining hall walls; lime-plastered cream secondary work against red sandstone. The 1789 Daniell river-side drawing also confirms the Yamuna lapping the ghats at the foot of the mound in that epoch.
- BOTH CONTAMINATION WARNINGS THE SURVEY RAISES ARE REAL — I verified each. srimandir.com's Jaipur Temple page literally opens 'Jaipur Temple, also known as the Radha Madan Mohan Temple, is one of the most famous temples in Vrindavan, Uttar Pradesh.' And the Commons description on the 1789 Daniell aquatint of the VRINDAVAN temple reads 'the Madan Mohan temple, built in the 17th century of brick and terracotta and covered in terracotta plaques in the Bengali manner' — a Bishnupur description attached to a red-sandstone Vrindavan building.

### Contamination risks

- VERIFIED: Wikimedia Commons' description on the 1789 Daniell aquatint of the Vrindavan temple says 'built in the 17th century of brick and terracotta and covered in terracotta plaques in the Bengali manner' — that is Bishnupur's Madan Mohan, pasted onto Vrindavan's picture. Anyone harvesting that file's metadata will get brick-and-terracotta material guidance for a red-sandstone building.
- VERIFIED: srimandir.com states 'Jaipur Temple, also known as the Radha Madan Mohan Temple'. The Jaipur Mandir / Radha Madhav is a separate 1916-17 building by Sawai Madho Singh II at Kishor Pura, ~930 m SSE, red sandstone and white marble, pillared halls. Nothing from it belongs on Dwadashaditya Tila.
- NEW: Wikipedia states 'In 1819 AD, Nanda Kumar Basu, a zamindar from Baharu, rebuilt the temple AT THE TOP OF THE HILL near the Yamuna river.' Growse's plate puts the new temple OUTSIDE the compound, across the street, as a small rectangle — and Growse dates it c.1821 under Nand Kumar Ghos. Do not let the Wikipedia phrasing put the new temple on the mound. brajrasik says 'at the foot of the hill'. Follow the plate.
- NEW: mathura.nic.in's 'stands at a height of 50 feet' is an ELEVATION, not a tower height, and is re-scraped by aggregators as though it were the building's height. The paired popular formula '60 foot high temple on a 50 foot hill' shows how the two numbers actually split. The survey inverted this and used 50 ft as a check on its sanctum-tower photogrammetry.
- NEW: Growse's own plate does not scale consistently with Growse's own text — the plate's shrine reads 15.2 ft against his stated 20 ft (or his 57 ft nave reads 74.9 ft against the plate's shrine). Any figure 'measured off the plate' carries ~30% scale ambiguity, not the ±8% the survey claims. This contaminates the whole DERIVED dimension block, and it is how the chapel ended up 25% too small.
- NEW: the Vrindavan Today restoration report is dated 19 May 2025 and speaks of grass 'atop the roof' as well as in the courtyard. Treating it as a ~2024 courtyard-only story both dates the paving wrong and buries live evidence that something on this temple is roofed today — against the survey's hard 'DO NOT roof the nave'.
- STANDING: the Commons category 'Madan Mohan Temple, Vrindavan' is clean in its current listing (I enumerated it — all 25 files are Vrindavan, Daniell, Seeta Ram or Growse's plate, no Bishnupur/Cooch Behar files), but the survey is right that keyword searches for 'Madan Mohan' outside the category pull Bishnupur, Cooch Behar, Karauli, Gokul, Ramkeli and two 19th-century Mathura-city Madan Mohans. Harvest by category and by ASI number N-UP-A197, never by name search.

## Built — 2026-10-02 (MadanMohanMandir.js)

What was here before: a 29.5 m spine of nave, choir and sanctum on flat
ground, turned 45 degrees, at the precinct's centroid — no mound, no gateway,
no second temple, the towers' characters right but nothing round them.

**Where and which way.** The origin is the centroid of OSM way 334671983 (the
precinct), and the box frame is turned so +lz is EAST and +lx NORTH. Every
outline is OSM's: the precinct (19 nodes), the temple (way 334671987, 88
nodes: nave lx 1.75-9.95 x lz 8.3-25.66, sanctum octagon centred (5.85, -2.1)
r 4.35, chapel octagon (-3.55, -2.15) r 4.3), the gateway tower (671678177,
11.8 x 12.2 m with its street porch and court-side recess), Sanatana Goswami's
bhajan kutir (334671984), his samadhi (334671985) and the second kutir
(768143156). ESRI z19 imagery (measurement only) puts the bastion block on the
NW lobe and the approach stair on the west face south of it, running due east
up a grass slope to the court at lx -6.5.

**The mound is geometry**, since the terrain is flat alluvium: the precinct's
own outline walled in battered brick (a 0.9 m batter to court height, banded
lifts, three string courses, rows of small square holes, a vented parapet);
the NW bastion block standing 2.3 m over the court with polygonal bastions at
its two west angles (2006, 2023); a grass slope with two salmon curbs between
the block and the stair, railed at its foot; the stair in two flights with a
landing between coped parapets; the ASI's blue board and its maroon "Madan
Mohan Temple" board at the foot (2023); a ledge 2.9 m below the court outside
the nave's east door — Growse's "drop of some 9 or 10 feet" — with the door
railed, so it cannot be walked out of.

**Heights, measured against the survey's towers.** With the chapel tower at
22 m and the sanctum tower at 14.5 m (the survey's photogrammetry), the 2006
approach photograph is fitted with its camera on the Parikrama Marg — the
place that puts both tower tops and the fence's foot where the photograph has
them (the camera position is fitted, not known). Against that fit the court's west wall
and the block's parapet came out 2.4 m and 3.0 m too high at a court of
11.5 m; the 2023 photograph from the stair's foot gives 1.9 m and 2.6 m. So
the court is 9.5 m over the lane and the block 2.3 m over the court. These are
still estimates tied to the towers' own (+-20 per cent), and say so.

**The temple.** The nave is a roofless shell of salvaged courses capped with
ragged brick (Growse p.252), 1.7 m walls, three square-headed openings a side
clustered east (the plate: 3.6 / 1.2 / 1.7 / 1.8 / 1.9 / 1.4 / 5.7), a cusped
niche over each lintel as relief only, two steps up to each. The choir is a
roofed stump with a barred arch from the nave. The sanctum tower is a plain
banded octagon with flat angle ribs, an egg profile and a blunt top with
nothing on it, one small high opening. The chapel tower is panelled over every
face — three columns of sunk squares per face, rosettes down the middle,
lozenges either side — with bead-string colonnettes, four bud-corbel
mouldings, a neck, a 24-lobed crown overhanging it and a small cap; its single
east door is barred under the inscription niche, three steps up.

**The gateway**: a banded block, a cornice of 64 pendant brackets, seven
receding courses with corner kutas, a short ridge; the court side a deep
barred arch, the street porch shut.

**The new temple** is built across the lane where Growse's plate draws it,
south-east of the gateway — its POSITION is the plate carried onto the
imagery's lane-side range, and its FORM (a plain two-storey haveli, an arched
doorway, three jharokhas, a hall, a gilt cusped screen before the sanctum) is
INFERRED. The altar group is the three of altars.js. This is the place's
interior: darshan, the bell, the night veil. The old nave is a `hall` room,
`quiet`, entered from its side openings.

**The compound** is the mound, and `also` (new) adds the west approach to the
Parikrama Marg, the samadhi, the lane along the south side out to the street
(r99427157), the new temple and the second kutir — so the town presses up to
the walls on the north, east and south as the survey says, and the lane stays
open to walk.

**Epoch 2024-25**: the court in red sandstone slabs (ASI, c. 2024) except the
south strip, a garden of planted beds in the imagery; the towers in the
survey's 2006-23 colours, before the 2025-26 cleaning and without the 2026
scaffold. The red-painted building at the stair's head (2023, 2026; absent in
2006) is included.

**Not built, queued:** the trees in the court north of the sanctum (imagery);
whatever stands south of the stair below the red building (the imagery's pale
roofs); the east approach Growse built in 1875; the walk-in choir and
sanctum; the gateway's passage.
